# Chapter Seven – When Faith Is Tested


“You can choose, Mister Oakmoore.”

Edward regarded Carden from across the rich cedarwood desk. A cloud passed outside and covered Edward’s features in afternoon shade. Carden clenched his armrests beneath the desk.

Edward placed a hand on a thin stack of documents. “Annul all these tax-exemptions and personally collect the past year’s arrears you deprived Parliament.”

Those words ached Carden’s stomach.

Perhaps because Carden did not react, Edward sighed and tapped another document.

“Or be terminated.”

Carden uttered a quick prayer to the Father in his heart before he spoke.

“If I choose termination, do the tax-exemptions still stand?”

Edward eyes glinted. “Don’t be a fool. I will annul them regardless. We’re discussing whether I can still count on you as a subordinate.”

Carden’s gut dropped to his feet.

“Why even go through this, then?”

When Edward’s eyes narrowed, Carden added. “Sir.”

Edward inhaled and then folded his arms across his chest.

“Because your father is an important partner of government. Because your father-in-law is a respected member of Parliament. And because…” Edward’s eyes turned to Carden’s right wrist and something flickered through them “… I will not deny a proven patriot his conscience.”

Carden sank a fraction lower into his seat. He gestured at the pile of tax-exemptions. “Is there no other way, sir?”

Edward’s features, even in the shade, softened. After a long pause, he said, “Tell me what I’m supposed to do as Alfainia’s latest Finance-Minister at a time Imperium Divinus is challenging the Commonwealth’s sovereignty around the world.”

Nothing but Portsmouth’s muffled, bustling activity outside answered.

“Can I take some time to think about this, sir?” Carden asked, reached out, and pulled both documents.

Edward waved his hand as if gesturing Carden to go. “You have until tomorrow morning.”

As Carden plodded to the door, Edward said after him, “I notified your father and father-in-law this morning. They promised me they will ensure this matter is settled.”

Carden nearly tripped.

“I pray you make the right decision, Carden.”

As if the weather itself turned on Carden, an unusual chill infused the autumn breeze that blew down Carden’s back the whole walk home. Despite the high afternoon sun—the first time in a year perhaps he walked home this early—that cold wind bit through Carden’s coat and stabbed his spine.

When Carden unlocked his flat’s front-door, Marelyn’s boots already sat on the shoe-rack and her outer coat hung on the coat-hanger. At least it made things easier. Carden found Marelyn in her personal study, staring blankly at the wall that mounted her collection of purses accumulated over their marriage. She clutched several papers in her hands so hard the edges crumpled.

Carden winced, braced his heart, and sat on the floor in front of Marelyn. He laid the six tax-exemptions and unsigned termination letter between them. No words, just space. His wife deserved at least that much.

Who knew how many minutes or hours passed before Marelyn’s lips cracked open.

“Our fathers sought me out earlier. A chauffeur picked me up at the Stock Exchange and brought me straight to your father’s Portsmouth penthouse for lunch.”

Marelyn placed the documents she held amongst Carden’s documents on the floor. An unsigned divorce form and an unsigned disinheritance form.

A strange clarity enveloped Carden even as Marelyn said afterwards while rolling her gold-and-silver wedding ring around her finger, “They asked me to convey a message.”

“There is no room for a disgraced prodigal son.”

Carden scooted forward, sat over the documents, and clasped Marelyn’s cold hands.

“What about you?”

Marelyn’s eyes finally left her wall of purses and rested on Carden’s. Where before she stared solemnly, now she blinked at him furiously.

“Could you give me the night alone?”

Carden’s chest lurched.

“Of course. Anything you need, dear.”

Carden pulled out the spare mattress, plopped it in the living room, and then threw on his coat and left with all haste to the street. Some invisible force propelled him from everything he ever knew.

It was easier not to think. Carden just let his feet take him wherever they willed. How bizarre then when Carden finally snapped back to reality he found himself before Saint Alfred’s Cathedral. So lost in thoughts it seemed that he passed through multiple boroughs and crossed the Rorsch River’s bridge without noticing. The sun tickled the tops of Northern Portsmouth’s surrounding factories, flats, foundries, and warehouses. Northern Portsmouth, home for a vast majority of Portsmouth’s residents segregated from Southern Portsmouth where Government and the affluent lived. Come tomorrow Carden probably counted himself amongst the former.

How fitting his feet already internalized his choice before his heart.

A lot more people clamored the pews today than the last time Carden visited with Marelyn and the Bakers at the turn of summer. Carden snuck down the side-aisle and couldn’t help but notice the variety of people tonight. A handful of men dressed as managers or lawyers sat alongside soot-covered laborers. Women with the fine bright-wool outerwear of Portsmouth’s middle-class seated amidst seamstresses, midwives, and housewives. All solemnly silent as a priest behind the altar recited something from an unfurled scroll.

A scroll that absolutely thrummed with a tiny pulse of divinity.

*Could it be?*

Carden took a seat on the middle-row’s outer edge, beside a bearded, middle-aged man dressed in rough workman overalls. The man clutched an empty alms bowl in restless hands. Another victim, it seemed, of the deteriorating economy. Carden fished out several one-*cone* coins and slipped them into the alms bowl.

“Thank you, kind sir.”

Carden nodded, but before he could turn to the liturgy the man patted Carden’s lap. The man asked in a quiet yet deep voice, “Your name, son?”

Carden stifled the sharp remark on his tongue. He was certainly not that much younger than the man next to him. Instead, Carden said, “Carden. Carden Oakmoore.”

Abruptly, a strong hand touched the back of Carden’s head and before Carden could react the man uttered quickly under his breath.

“May the Father bless you, Carden Oakmoore.”

Tears moistened Carden’s eyes unbidden. Carden snagged the man’s sleeve as the man rose.

“And yours, good sir?” Carden asked.

The man flickered a smile underneath his thick beard. His words came out as a soft whisper.

“Alfred.”

Common enough name in Northern Portsmouth.

Alone now on this side of the pew, Carden listened intently to the sermon. A teaching about resilience amidst intense persecution. A dangerous subject in these times, yet Carden marveled how the priest recited the *Scripture’s* message with candor. Not to mention how the crowd drank it all in without fear.

Or anger.

Just calm acceptance from everyone around him. Whether his imagination or reality, Carden could not tell. Yet his body grew lighter and things slowly came off just a tad bit sharper to his mind. Such that when the sermon ended and the congregation dispersed, Carden approached the altar and the *Scripture’s* ever-more palpable divinity.

“May I?” Carden asked and flickered his Magic through his eyes for the priest to see.

The priest, a man so old he stooped terribly and constantly readjusted his too-large robes with gaunt arms, smiled in response and beckoned Carden to proceed.

Carden beheld the unfurled vellum scroll. Nothing about its divinity made him feel miniscule the way the God of War’s or Creator’s *Scriptures* did. Just palpable…

Reassurance.

Carden placed both hands on the *Scripture* and no force slammed him or whisked him away to realms unknown. Just a thin, ephemeral male voice that spoke from inside him as his eyes followed the glowing Arcane Tongue runes.

“*What father when his son asks for bread gives him a snake?  
What mother when her daughter asks for counsel gives her rebuke?  
If even your parents in this world know what is good for you,  
how much more your Father above who sees all?*”

Carden pulled his hands back with featherlight gentleness.

“Did Saint Alfred write this?” Carden asked the priest. To hear words from such a man, even if only through *Scripture*, Carden could weep. After all, Saint Alfred vanished from the face of the world more than five-decades ago during Alfainia’s liberation from Imperial occupation. Ostensibly because he felt betrayed Alfainia turned to the God of War for its freedom. No other chance to find the man’s words. 

The priest shook his head and said, “No. Alfred Essex was but the humble messenger. He received this directly from the Father and bequeathed it to us when this cathedral was first built.”

Carden nearly fainted. Something clicked inside of him.

“Thank you, holy father.” Carden bowed in the Essexian Church’s rite and hurried away. He sat at the foremost pew and uttered a quick prayer.

“Father in Heaven, please provision Marelyn and I for what comes.”

As Carden left the cathedral, he paused in the front-garden. Saint Alfred’s statue stood dark now this late in the evening. Yet the polished surface still reflected light from flats, streetlights, and other nearby buildings. The statue’s beard and alms bowl gleamed particularly bright in the darkness and looked impeccably familiar.

*It couldn’t be…*

Carden knew what to do now. He opened his front-door right as midnight bells tolled once throughout the city. Marelyn sat on the couch facing the dark hearth, unperturbed it seemed by the flat’s chill. Carden came over, lit the hearth, warmed his hands, and then sat next to Marelyn. He clasped Marelyn’s frigidly cold hands and hoped the warmth in his seeped across.

“Where did you go?” Marelyn asked in a perfectly flat voice. Her puffed, red, dry eyes spoke otherwise.

“Saint Alfred’s Cathedral,” Carden answered.

“I see. And what’s your decision?”

Carden squeezed Marelyn’s hands and inhaled deeply. Here it went.

“Termination.”

Marelyn’s eyes suddenly blinked rapidly at Carden and then her cheeks twitched.

“I see.”

Marelyn’s words came out as a sob. Her hands smoothly slid free of Carden’s. For some odd reason Carden felt as if some piece of him just fell off.

Carden blurted out, “What do you think, Marelyn? Should I?”

Tears dripped down Marelyn’s cheeks. “Should you? I’m sorry, Carden. I’m so sorry. I just can’t. I just can’t right now.”

Marelyn bolted off the couch, wrapped her arms around herself, and locked herself in her personal study. Carden’s hand fell on the indentation Marelyn left on the couch’s leather.

*I guess this is the end of our marriage. We had a good run.*

Marelyn never left her study. Carden tucked himself to bed on the spare mattress, that way if Marelyn came out she could sleep comfortably in the master bedroom without the trouble of his company. Yet long into the night, the only things that emerged from Marelyn’s study were sobs, sniffles, and wails.

Marelyn never answered when Carden knocked on the door next morning. The silence indicated either she was truly fed up with him or finally fell asleep. Regardless, no reason he needed to disturb her further. He made a breakfast of scratch omelets over reheated croissants and left a plate for Marelyn.

Carden entered Edward’s office to a morning sun that just started to peek over Southern Portsmouth’s skyline in the window behind Edward’s desk. Edward’s side of the office with the cedarwood desk remained draped in the skyline and wall’s shade, whereas Carden stood by the door bathed in the sun’s gentle rays.

Edward seated and silhouetted by the morning sun never bade Carden sit. Carden too felt the situation demanded he stand at attention like a proper soldier.

“Your decision?” Edward asked without preamble or greeting.

Carden took out the termination letter from his briefcase, signed last night, and respectfully placed it before Edward.

“Very well then,” Edward said and signed the form.

A silence dragged on that Carden assumed meant he could go. Carden turned and at that exact moment Edward’s voice rang behind him.

“For the public record, why would an esteemed civil-servant and decorated soldier undermine Parliament’s interests?”

Carden stopped, turned back, and placed a hand over his heart. He said in Arcane Tongue.

“Because I believe in the Father, and the Father teaches us love is the greatest commandment. I cannot in good faith ignore the suffering of my fellow man and still call myself a devout believer of His teachings.”

Carden paused as another thought struck. He added in Arcane Tongue.

“I gave my blood on the front-lines for the country Saint Alfred believed in. I did what he would have done.”

Edward stared on with an implacable expression. Finally, although the man never rose from his seat, he said in Arcane Tongue.

“From the bottom of my heart, I thank you for your service to Alfainia, Carden Oakmoore. May you enjoy your deserved rest.”

“You are dismissed.”

More than twenty years Carden walked through Commonwealth Exchequer for a living. Almost half his life. Now, as he trundled out with his belongings in a box, every hall, every colleague’s face, every chipped tile and scuffed paint looked surreal. Every step down the stairs to the lobby oddly lifted his spirits. Didn’t matter people glanced at him and he heard whispers behind him. By the time he exited the steel-and-glass tower, he felt…

Free.

Chef Wheatfield at Wheatfield’s Bistro lamented Carden’s termination and gave Carden a glorious lunch free-of-charge. Carden gobbled down the fare with gusto and asked Chef Wheatfield if his generosity meant Carden could expect discounts for future meals. Chef Wheatfield froze, gave him a twitching side-eye, and then reminded Carden the Wheatfield family needed to make a living too.

Fair enough.

The folks at Personnel kindly gave Carden two-days to vacate his civil-servant’s flat. Plenty of time to pack. Hopefully it didn’t take too long to get Marelyn’s… things…

Out.

Clean. His flat of more than twenty years looked spotless. The furniture that came with it still there, naturally. However, the knickknacks and accessories that marked Carden and Marelyn’s life together for the past two decades, all gone. Carden shuffled to the dining table where a document sat as if waiting for him to come home.

The disinheritance form, signed by one ‘Julian Oakmoore’.

Funny, the form triggered the same emotion in Carden as reading the morning papers. Rather, his heart throbbed at the other document he expected but couldn’t find.

Divorce papers with Marelyn’s signature on it.

Carden went to Marelyn’s study and found it unlocked. He cracked it open and turned on the lights. The wall that housed Marelyn’s collection of purses stood immaculate. Even the adhesive shelves that displayed the purses vanished. Someone even touched up with new paint the scuff marks those adhesive shelves would have left behind when peeled off.

Same with the study’s bookshelves, drawers, and trunks where Marelyn stored her things. Carden went to the bedroom and found the same. His things neatly folded and stacked as if Marelyn helped him pack. But her things, from clothes to sheets to accessories. All gone.

“Guess she spent so long packing she’s only just started filing the divorce papers at court,” Carden said, chuckling to himself.

The moment those words left Carden’s lips something inside him cracked. Reality finally sank in. Phlegm trickled down his throat, tears stung his eyes, and his body shuddered. Carden’s legs gave out, his back slid down the wall, and he sat with legs curled up and face buried against his knees.

Carden wept. He bawled, sobbed, and groaned. His eyes stung, his lungs kept hitching, and his hands and feet kept shaking.

No job, no family, no support.

Worse, the one person by his side for more than twenty years, gone. Possibly with all his money too, since he never bothered keeping up with the household accounts.

Just him and the world now.

Carden’s stomach groaned and that unfortunately yanked him back to life. How bold of his stomach to remind him he still existed.

“I should eat.” Carden grumbled to his feet and only then noticed dusk’s rich, deep rays shafting through the windows. No wonder his back and shoulders ached something fierce.

Thankfully Marelyn hadn’t cleaned out the pantry. Carden fell into the mechanical, mindless motions of making a stir-fry for dinner. No point rationing their expensive cooking oil, choice smoked meats, or other imported spices. Not the things one packed for eviction.

Carden divested his anger, his bitterness, his despair into the poor carrots, celery, garlic, ginger, mutton, and onions whose hapless fate landed them on his chopping board. Next, an entire block of salted butter went to the pot of creamiest risotto he ever made. No need to stretch butter for days anymore. Finally, he sliced their remaining potatoes and deep-fried the whole lot with the leftover cooking oil.

Wait, he made too much. He couldn’t possibly finish all this—

“Why does eviction smell so sublime?”

Warm arms wrapped Carden’s belly from behind and a chin rested on his shoulder.

“Marelyn?” Carden asked before his mind caught up. His heart pounded when images of divorce-papers and alimonies flitted past.

*No. Tonight at least, I’d like to enjoy a simple dinner one last time.*

“Let’s eat,” Carden said and it surprised him how calm those words came out.

“I would love nothing more,” Marelyn said with a strange lyricism in her voice.

Carden brought the platters out and studiously avoided the documents Marelyn left on the dining-table. Yet he still spotted a second set of disinheritance papers over the first. Incredible, was Julian that petty he needed to legally disinherit Carden twice over?

Marelyn cleared the table, placed the exquisite indigo purse Horace bought her last year to the side, and for some odd reason she moved with a certain languid grace. In fact, her entire countenance glowed and beggared question. She was smiling to herself and her green eyes sparkled. She must have won a tremendous amount of alimony from him at court.

Marelyn sat, propped her elbow on the table, and rested her cheek on her hand as she watched Carden. Their wedding-ring still glinted on Marelyn’s ring-finger.  At least she was still considerate enough to keep up the pretense one last time.

“Sorry I was home so late,” Marelyn said and pulled her platter over. Her face looked delighted at the first crunch of fries and the first sip of risotto.

Carden ate and everything tasted so bland with all this anxiety clogging his stomach. He did his best to avoid Marelyn’s eyes and dwell on just the food, yet something felt off. Marelyn came off a little too cheery for what they were about to finalize.

“You look great,” Carden said and wrangled the next words out. “Everything went well for you?”

Carden forced himself to look Marelyn in the eye for this.

“Lovely, in fact!” Marelyn beamed as she scarfed down the risotto. “Packing our things went faster than I expected. And I got a lovely deal for my purses.”

Packing *our* things. A lovely deal for—wait… what?

Carden blinked at Marelyn, wondering if he’d finally gone stark raving mad under catastrophe.

“And the look on father’s face was priceless when we filed the disinheritance form at court.”

*Excuse me?*

“Marelyn, I…” Carden fumbled and spat out his risotto when no words came fore. “What?”

Only then did his mind really see the second disinheritance form. Disinheritance from the Winston family. *Winston*. Signed by one Charles Winston.

Marelyn beamed the smuggest smile Carden ever saw on her face. She said, “Father refused to let the family name get tarnished. He explicitly said…” Marelyn’s voice deepened to an impersonation of a male voice not like Charles’s at all “… if you insist on staying with a civil-servant terminated in disgrace and disowned by his own family, then feel free to join him in those circumstances.”

Carden blinked at Marelyn and then at the wedding-ring still on her finger.

“You’re not… you don’t want to divorce me?” Carden asked.

Marelyn’s cheerful countenance morphed into a deep scowl. “Why in the Father’s name would I divorce you?”

Carden dropped his utensils with a loud *clang*. Nothing made sense.

“Last night! You just locked yourself in your study and said nothing at all. Yesterday you brought home unsigned divorce papers. Today I came home to a flat scraped clean. What else was I supposed to think?”

Marelyn’s lips opened and closed without any words and her eyes darted between Carden’s eyes and Carden’s chest. Finally, the smile on her wavered as if something fought to leave her lips.

When Marelyn finally spoke, she said, “I’m so sorry. I was too devastated.”

Marelyn set her utensils down, gripped the table’s edge, and inhaled deeply. A moment later her expression turned resolute and she pulled over her disinheritance papers. “From the moment our fathers told me what happened yesterday, my heart already knew what we should do.”

Marelyn pulled over her blue purse next and clenched the straps with both hands until her knuckles whitened. “Except it wasn’t ready yet for the consequences.”

“When you were at Saint Alfred’s, I prayed all night you’d choose to keep your job. I prayed you would give me an excuse not to do the right thing.”

Marelyn let go of the purse and clasped her hands as if in prayer. She pressed her lips against them, her eyes glistened at Carden, and she said, “Except my dear husband, you didn’t.”

“You made the right choice.”

“We made the right choice.”

Marelyn’s nose reddened and she sniffled. “And I wasn’t ready to face reality. I’m sorry I worried you. Carden, I’m so sorry I left you alone.”

Marelyn suddenly folded her hands over the table and gave Carden an unvarnished look at her face.

“But Carden, I was never going to divorce you.”

“Especially not after I told you to approve Annie and Roger’s latest case. I promised.”

Carden nearly fell over his chair in relief. Marelyn was not leaving. She joined him in the trenches of this disaster. He wasn’t going at this alone.

The Father truly blessed him with Marelyn. Hopefully he could repay her one day.

Carden reached out and cupped Marelyn’s hot hands in his. She squeezed back with an unyielding grip.

“Marelyn,” Carden said and paused, suddenly unsure how to describe all he felt about her in words. Marelyn’s rebuke about him complicating things came to mind. So he kept it simple.

“Thank you.”

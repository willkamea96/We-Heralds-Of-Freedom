# Chapter Eight - When Faith Is Rewarded


Carden winced at the barrage of questions his old friends and classmates threw his way. Before he could snap at them, Marelyn gave his hand a reassuring squeeze under the table. She flashed an awkward smile and rolled her green eyes at his—no, *their*—friends.

“Look, I know how bad this is.” Carden straightened his plain leather jacket’s collar for emphasis. “But in case you all failed to notice, I’m unemployed. You’ll need to find someone else in Government to trouble.”

Nothing but the café’s normal morning activity answered him. Horse-drawn omnibuses clattered and creaked past, neighboring parties grumbled or laughed, and Windbury’s gentle autumn sunlight amplified the disappointment on the six faces across Carden.

“Carden,” Annie finally said in a low tone that stirred some unpleasant distant memories in Carden. “How can you be joking about this?”

Marelyn leaned forward and leveled Annie an unamused look. “Annie, Carden and I are disinherited, homeless, and unemployed. Forgive us if we try to bring levity into our misery.”

Carden’s spine tingled fiercely from whatever crackled between the two women.

“Now, now,” Roger said with an equally-worried expression and nudged Annie back from the edge of her seat. Thank goodness it wasn’t just Carden who felt it. “I know everyone’s tense, but please, let’s focus on the problem at hand.”

“Agreed,” Carden said without hesitation. Better he dealt with Roger than Annie in this instance. At least Roger as a fellow veteran went straight to the point. “The principle question is how we will keep your philanthropy going and how Marelyn and I can rebuild.”

“I can help with accommodations,” Roger said and gestured the point aside. “You can rent the old flat Annie and I used to live in.”

Carden almost blurted out a thank you before a key detail crossed his mind. He flashed Marelyn a worried look. “Do we have enough money?”

Marelyn hid a laugh and how was it possible she looked both amused and incredulous. “Did you ever consider our finances before submitting your resignation?”

Carden’s face burned.

Marelyn laughed louder and rubbed Carden’s lap. “What would you do without me? Yes, we have money. I sorted everything out.”

Carden kissed Marelyn’s knuckles for their friends to see. “Father bless your heart, my love. He knows how unworthy I am of your magnificence.”

Not sure why Marelyn blushed, but Carden went back to the subject at hand.

“So how are your cash flows right now?”

Roger and the others either squirmed in their seats or turned rigid. Annie said in a very curt tone, “We had enough to pay our arrears to the Customs and Revenue Board, but we’re severely short for the year’s remaining charity-work.”

Carden’s friend Jericho, a middle-aged balding man who owned a textile factory, said, “At the current demand for welfare, we won’t have enough to cover winter which, unfortunately, is when demand peaks.”

“And when people are most vulnerable,” Carden’s old classmate Mary, a petite brunette who ran a pharmaceutical distribution company with her husband, said right after.

Marelyn skimmed through their various books again with boggling speed. She asked after a moment, “Have you all discussed this with Mayor Horace? Sounds like a gap the municipal budget could probably help you bridge.”

If Horace even secured additional funding from Central after the debacle Carden caused. Carden’s friends shot each other awkward looks. What Horace warned Carden about last week rang in Carden’s ears. What a mess.

“You all realize he *is* mayor,” Carden said and ensured his tone brooked no doubt he meant that as a statement, “and thus ultimately responsible for Windbury’s welfare.”

Annie balled her hands into fists on the table. She spoke in a loud tone despite how Roger tried to quiet her down.

“Like how he dealt with last year’s riots?”

No soldiers patrolled Windbury’s streets now, yet a few nearby tables glanced their way with dark looks. Carden winced when Annie shrugged off Roger’s ministrations with a severe disposition.

“He cares more for you all and for Windbury than you realize,” Carden finally said. And to Roger specifically, the only other Arcanist in their group, Carden added in Arcane Tongue, “Horace was ready to support your welfare program in Government had he known ahead of time.”

Roger’s eyes widened and the man translated for the group without peeling his gaze from Carden. Carden’s friends all leaned back in their seats in silence. Finally, Carden’s old classmate Newton, a lanky richly-haired man who ran a shoe factory, said, “We’ve made a severe lapse of judgment excluding Horace, then. Carden, you and him have history. Maybe you could advocate on our behalf?”

“What would I be advocating for exactly?” Carden asked.

Roger cleared his throat and stared intently at Carden and Marelyn. “I’m afraid in this instance we will have to defer to both of your judgments. None of us are familiar with Government. We can only trust you will do right on us.”

Carden and Marelyn remained at the café after everyone left. Roger and Annie promised to get their old flat tidied up by the evening for Carden and Marelyn to move in. So Carden brainstormed ideas with Marelyn, yet an hour passed and they still felt nothing quite hit the mark. Carden threw his hands in the air at last and said, “I’m of the mood for a mindless walk. Care to join?”

Marelyn flickered a smile as she finished her sweet-butter croissant.

“I’d like nothing better.”

They walked hand-in-hand through Windbury. Down streets where half the businesses remained shuttered and boarded-up if not outright still left as charred husks after last year’s riots. Carden and Marelyn passed lines of ragged men and women in working-class attire wrapped around cathedral and church soup-kitchens. The two of them skirted craters in streets and damaged sidewalks, unrepaired still a year later.

Away from Windbury and its dreary scars for a respite in the sparse green countryside.

The late autumn sun crept to its day’s apex right as Carden and Marelyn crested the grassy hill. Below them, rolling meadows and pasturelands stretched until they abutted Windbury’s outer suburbs. From here they saw Windbury in all its splendor, a quaint, sprawling provincial capital nestled between hills and the sea.

Home for the foreseeable future.

Carden threw his coat over the grass and invited Marelyn to sit on it so as not to dirty her trousers. He himself sat on the grass and clutched his knees as memories of this place came back.

“Annie and I came here a lot when we were teenagers,” Carden said the first words that came to mind. After everything they just went through, he hoped to just ramble. A cool breeze embraced them. Carden inhaled the grassy scent and soaked in the air’s refreshing touch.

“Our private little nook from the world, where we could daydream away from life’s worries.” Carden laced his fingers between Marelyn’s. “Now it’s ours.”

Marelyn leaned on Carden’s arm and rested her cheek on his shoulder. She said nothing and Carden gave her the space. Carden contented himself with the meditative sensation of her rhythmic breathing and the whistling breeze’s gentle caress.

When Marelyn finally spoke, her voice carried a very wistful tone. “While you and your old flame gallivanted out here, I nagged a certain young baker at his family’s downtown bakery for free croissants every morning. Hazelnut-cream ones were a particular treat, and coincidentally the most expensive.”

Carden chuckled and Marelyn ribbed his side playfully. Marelyn snuggled her cheek deeper into Carden’s shoulder. “What did it say about me back then that I was instantly smitten by someone who indulged me without asking for anything in return?”

Carden snaked his arm around Marelyn’s back and held her tight. “Probably what people said about me when I promised to marry the first person who joined my daydreams.”

The mild breeze coiled and swirled a scattering of fallen red leaves around them.

“Carden,” Marelyn said when the breeze subsided and the leaves drifted back to the ground.

“Let’s make this work.”

A hurricane of memories flitted past. Carden pressed Marelyn tight against his side.

“Let’s make this work.”

When Carden and Marelyn finally arrived at Horace’s mayoral office for their late afternoon appointment, the two of them finally hashed out an acceptable proposal.

“It’s a gamble,” Marelyn said in the carriage-ride. “It’s the most pragmatic option, but Horace is a stickler for rules.”

Carden butted the back of his head against the carriage’s frame. “I better be my most charming with Horace, then. If you could help me butter him up even more that would be lovely.”

Marelyn scoffed with a smile and rolled her eyes.

Horace stared out the window while leaning against the window-frame when Carden and Marelyn finally entered his office. Official documents scattered on desks, more red ink than blue scrawled across whiteboards, and many empty, coffee-stained cups on conference tables and counters. The smell of old paper, stale coffee, lingering cigar-smoke, and an undertone of sweat emphasized Horace’s circumstances.

“I’m sorry for what happened, Carden,” Horace said without turning away from his contemplation of whatever snagged his attention outside. “If it’s any consolation, I tried to show Edward what you did accomplished miracles for Windbury’s people.”

Horace finally turned to Carden and Marelyn. Carden nearly flinched at the sight of the deep bags under Horace’s eyes. Horace said in a flat, almost resigned tone, “Shame you and I must endure these times after everything we suffered on the mainland.”

Horace’s eyes brightened a touch at Marelyn’s rich-indigo purse. “Marelyn, I’m touched you still have the purse.”

Marelyn smiled and spoke in a genial tone even as she surreptitiously nudged Carden’s back. “If we are enduring these times, we shouldn’t take the kindness of others for granted, no? Besides, you have such impeccable taste. This purse goes so well with my wardrobe.”

Horace’s smile finally reached his eyes. He gestured to his desk and the three of them sat. Carden inwardly uttered a prayer of profound gratitude to the Father for Marelyn’s presence in his life.

“To what do I owe the pleasure?” Horace asked and praise the Father he sounded like he truly meant it.

Carden girded his loins. Here it went. 

“Horace, we were wondering if City Hall would be open to collaborating with several well-meaning private enterprises to tide Windbury’s citizens through these current times. Bakers For You, Jericho’s Clothiers, Mary’s Pharmacy, and three others are running a citywide charity scheme to…”

And so Carden went over his and Marelyn’s proposal with Horace. Clothing, food, medicine, shoes, and other auxiliary services diverted from preexisting manufacturing or distribution channels for welfare. Partially subsidized by the municipal budget to make up the gap once covered by tax-exemptions. Welfare qualification conditional on recipients volunteering as public servants to restore property damage still unrepaired a year after the riots.

That last part, Carden and Marelyn came up with. Perhaps Annie, Roger, and the rest believed charity needed to be unconditional. Carden and Marelyn believed if someone could contribute to the city helping them, they should.

“Not everyone complicit in last year’s riots were brought to justice,” Horace said with a frown. “You’re asking City Hall to extend alms knowing some recipients were convicts who evaded the law?”

Carden shook his head. “These are people pushed to desperation because they’ve lost livelihoods, not pathological convicts.”

*At least, I hope we already arrested all the pathological ones.*

“Marelyn and I are asking City Hall to extend grace, indebt said suspects to the hand that feeds them, and incentivize them to atone for their crimes.” Carden replaced Horace’s mug of coffee with a glass of clear water. Carden then pointed at Horace’s Black Hounds wrist-tattoo with his eyes. “Remember our time on the mainland? Punitive justice only went so far to pacify a hostile populace. At a certain point we needed to transition to rehabilitative justice to properly secure local collaboration.”

Carden hated the notion of framing his hometown in such a manner, and from the corner of his eye even Marelyn sat stiffer after he spoke. Unfortunately, Carden lacked a better framework for Horace.

Horace’s chair creaked as he leaned back and regarded Carden and Marelyn. After a strangely drawn out pause, Horace asked, “And who will be supervising all these volunteers? We can’t just have unvetted work-gangs rummaging through the city.”

No turning back.

“Marelyn and I will.”

Horace frowned at Carden. “Excuse me?”

Carden wrapped his arm around a sheepish Marelyn’s shoulder and grinned at Horace. “Marelyn and I are currently unemployed. We do need to eat, and we are familiar with money and government. We’d be happy to work under you as civil servants and manage this initiative.”

Carden leaned forward and gestured at the bags under Horace’s eyes. “Why not offer our services to a friend in need?”

Horace tilted his chair back on its two rear-legs and roared with laughter.

“Carden, you scallywag!” Horace wagged a finger at Carden. “How dare you and Marelyn ride on the coattails of your selfless friends to sneak job applications in!”

Horace’s mirth faded a touch and he turned to Marelyn. “But, Marelyn, why would you stoop to such work? I couldn’t possibly allow a woman from old nobility to muck with the masses, nor should you be seen doing so.”

Marelyn chuckled warmly even though Carden noticed her hands clenched her trousers under the desk. “I’ve walked away from all that. Disowned, matter-of-fact.”

Marelyn leaned towards Horace and batted her blonde eyelashes at him. “Horace, I would deeply appreciate anything you could do to help Carden and I get back on our feet.”

Horace briefly blushed but then regarded Carden and Marelyn with a very sober expression. He clasped his hands together, twiddled his thumbs, and his eyes took on a faraway look. Horace eventually pushed himself off his seat and fetched a bulging folder from a nearby cabinet. He placed it before Carden and Marelyn.

“Consider your proposal approved. I’ll notify your friends personally. You both start tomorrow.”

Horace rubbed Carden and Marelyn’s shoulders in a strong grip.

“Welcome to the trenches. Have fun.”

After Carden and Marelyn signed their employment contracts with Horace, they took their rented carriage to an older-looking block of flats downtown in the late afternoon. Annie and Roger showed the fifth-story flat off to Carden and Marelyn while the four Bakers teenagers shuttled Carden and Marelyn’s trunks in. The place could have been better.

“We’ve tidied the place up as best we could,” Roger said as he ran a hand along one of the walls. Carden curled his lip at the peeling wallpaper. Thank the Father though he neither sniffed nor spotted mold or mildew underneath. Roger shot a complicated smile when Carden said nothing and said, “But this was already an old flat when we first got it twenty-some years ago.”

Annie walked Marelyn over to the curtained double-bed at the corner and patted the mattress to show its springiness. Annie said, “We replaced all the furniture just for you both, so don’t worry about that.”

Marelyn laid a hand on Annie’s shoulder and said, “Thank you.”

“The building itself is sound,” Roger said and tapped the walls which returned solid *thumps*. “You’ll just have to put up with the fact it’s old.”

“But it has showers and piped hot water!” Annie added with a wide smile and popped open the washroom’s creaking door.

Carden filed age as the least of this flat’s problems. This place compressed bedroom, living-room, dining-room, kitchen, and pantry into *one* common living space. Practically reduced individual privacy to just the washroom. By his estimate, this unit probably spanned less than half the size of his old civil-service flat. No wonder Annie and Roger moved out as soon as they welcomed their first child. He and Marelyn would have to learn to be a lot more comfortable with each other in such intimate space.

Carden glanced at Marelyn. Although Marelyn’s face looked cordial before Annie and Roger, her smile lingered even when Annie and Roger turned away.

*I suppose that won’t be such a bad thing.*

“Thank you,” Carden finally said and patted Roger’s arm. “We appreciate it, truly.”

Roger shook his head with a smile while Annie abruptly squeezed Carden in a hug. “No, thank you. I can’t believe you two convinced Horace Livingstone.”

“This is the least we can do for you, Carden, Marelyn. You both are family now.”

Roger hugged Marelyn and said, “If either of you need anything, you know where to find us.”

“Just don’t forget rent,” Roger said and Marelyn slapped the man’s arm with a laugh.

When the Bakers finally left, Carden and Marelyn stood at the center of their new flat. Carden wrapped his arm around Marelyn’s hip and pressed her tight against him. She leaned her cheek on his shoulder.

“We’ll make this work.”

Carden flinched and turned to Marelyn. The wide-eyed look she gave back confirmed it. How impeccable they said those words together without meaning too.

Carden and Marelyn laughed and finally started unpacking their things into their new home.

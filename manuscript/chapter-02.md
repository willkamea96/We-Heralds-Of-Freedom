# Chapter Two – Home Is Not Home


“You cannot allow this, sir!”

Carden clasped his hands over his belly and leaned back on his cold leather chair until it creaked. The balding, middle-aged man across the desk from him grew redder the longer Carden dragged the silence out. No fault of Carden’s office, he already propped the window open and invited the cool morning’s winter breeze in for his petitioners’ comfort.

“Mister Lacklace—”

The middle-aged man slapped his fat thigh. “*Laplace*!”

It took everything to not laugh when that expression of anger jiggled the pudgy man’s jowls.

Carden already suffered too many forgettable faces and names this morning. “Mister Laplace, I understand your concerns, truly I do. But how am I to present your case in a compelling light? Your furniture firm is not by any means a strategic enterprise for the upcoming war effort, you posted consecutively higher net profits for the past three years, and you enjoy thriving exports to our great country’s growing Dominions abroad.” 

Carden plastered on his warmest smile and folded his hands over his lap. “Do enlighten me as to why you so desperately need tax exemptions, please.”

Laplace’s face flattened into a calmness made eerier under the office’s sunlight. Carden narrowed his eyes at the man.

“Mister Oakmoore, I grew profits the past three years by slashing our firm’s workforce. It was either that or our firm’s shareholders dumping our stocks. My sales revenue has been on the decline for the past three years.”

Wait a minute, that did not sound right. Laplace’s books clearly indicated growing exports and…

*This **slimy** little thief.*

“Mister Laplace, either you just blatantly lied to me about your finances, or you just confessed to the crime of falsifying a publicly-traded company’s accounting records. Which is it?”

Laplace’s flinch, silence, and blanching face admitted everything. Ah, so the man assumed Carden never scrutinized his financial records before this meeting. Hubris the bane of fools indeed.

Carden flexed his Arcanist powers as a sky-blue aura that flickered out his body like ghostly flames. Laplace’s face grew corpse-pale yet Carden pressed harder. Carden forced his will onto the surrounding Aether, made it infuse his voice with Zeal Magic, and brought it crashing down on Laplace’s head.

“How dare you come into my office and beg relief to hide financial fraud!”

Laplace recoiled in his seat yet Carden meant the show for something else. Sure enough, iron-soled boots clacked on tiles outside. Two tall tax officers in dark-blue uniforms barged into Carden’s office, batons and cuffs ready.

“This is a misunderstanding!” Laplace exclaimed as he wrestled against the officers. “I committed no fraud. I demand to speak to my lawyer!”

Yet the man’s voice, body language, and darting eyes betrayed how Carden’s Zeal Magic seeped in and magnified Laplace’s seed of doubt. A truthful man did not need to doubt his righteousness.

“We will let your lawyer know where to find you.” Carden passed Laplace’s file to one of the officers. “Audit his firm. I suspect it’s underreported taxes for the past three years.”

The bearded, grim-faced officer nodded while his peer wrangled Laplace out.

“No!”

Carden huffed a cigar, stared out the window, and made the most of this brief respite. Seemed like no more petitioners queued outside his office from the absence of knocks on his door. Hopefully that display with Laplace frightened any other frauds away.

What a mess. With Laplace’s included, that made it three firms this morning alone at risk of insolvency or drastic downsizing because of Edward’s tax hike three months ago. Maybe higher taxes did some good in that respect. Greedy businessowners rich off questionable financial practices finally reaped their just rewards.

“Your office sure is entertaining these days.”

Carden rolled his eyes, stifled his sigh, and doused his cigar as Horace Livingstone slunk into his office. The chestnut-haired man went straight to the filing shelf and used its glass panel as a mirror to adjust his white suit and tie. The man’s sleeve briefly fell and revealed a snarling black hound tattooed on his wrist.

Carden rubbed his own wrist’s matching military tattoo.

“I think we can both agree it’s still much preferrable to what we suffered back on the mainland.”

Horace paused, the faint reflection of his eyes on the shelf’s glass panel distant. “Sure is.”

Carden tossed his cigar out the window and unstoppered a bottle of apple cider. He poured two glasses and offered one to Horace. “Congratulations on winning the election. How’s it feel to be Mayor of Windbury?”

Horace grinned and clinked Carden’s toast. “I was expecting more of a reaction from you, since it’s your hometown we’re talking about.”

“Home is where you make it,” Carden said and chugged his glass dry. “Windbury hasn’t been home in a long time, and I trust even you aren’t so blithe as to muck it up.”

“I can’t tell if I should be flattered or offended, but no matter,” Horace said and placed his empty glass right over Carden’s notes. Carden clenched his teeth and moved the glasses to a dish-stand. While Carden tidied up his notes, Horace sat on the desk and shot Carden a mischievous look.

“I ran into Marelyn at the Stock Exchange this morning.”

If Horace expected some reaction from Carden, well the man fell far short.

“I can’t believe you let your wife run unchaperoned through that roughhouse. I just had to step in and fulfill a gentleman’s duty on your behalf.”

Carden snickered. “I appreciate it. Did Marelyn pay you a fair wage for your time?”

Horace beamed. “More than fair! We had a lovely brunch after and she was smitten when I bought her a new purse.”

How could someone like Horace be so brilliant and yet so daft when it came to women? Well, at least Horace indulged Marelyn’s thirst for high fashion and thus spared Carden’s pocketbooks.

“Although I dare say it’s not fitting for a woman like her to be so headstrong and inquisitive. You encourage her too much, old chap. I counseled her earlier she would accomplish a lot more good pouring all that energy into her home. The Father knows you and Marelyn are much deprived without children to care for. She wholeheartedly agreed and asked I air these concerns with you.”

Carden paused. Marelyn… was going to be insufferable tonight. Horace uttered the precise words she *never* wanted to hear. If Marelyn asked Horace to confide this with Carden, it meant she wanted *him* to dress Horace down on her behalf.

“Horace, old friend, let’s not be too friendly with other people’s spouses, yes?” Carden turned to Horace and patted the man’s muscled arm. “I do not want to hear marital advice from a man thrice divorced. Marelyn and I are more than happy with the way things are. Why don’t we talk about your new posting in Windbury. How familiar are you with the place?”

Carden just needed Horace to be content as Marelyn’s doting shopping fund without the drama.

After a long lunch at Wheatfield’s Bistro with Horace, Carden plodded back into his office and sank into the leather chair. Not good, the past three months of stress started to show in his waistline. How could the capital of a country with overseas Dominions on all seven continents lack healthier outlets of stress?

Didn’t help Horace becoming mayor meant he was no longer a fellow Special Commissioner of Income Taxes. Added even more to Carden’s compounding girth.

“Excuse me?” a dark-haired, middle-aged woman knocked on Carden’s door and entered with a similarly dark-haired, middle-aged man, presumably her husband. Both wore sharp brown business coats and brown wool caps that matched their eyes, good taste if a bit rustic. Ah, plain silver rings on their ring-fingers.

Strange, the woman struck him oddly familiar.

Carden forced out a smile, rose, and beckoned the couple to sit across him. “Welcome, to whom do I owe the pleasure?”

Just the way they sat spoke volumes. The husband leaned on Carden’s desk while the wife sat ramrod straight. Darn, another complicated problem.

“Roger and Annie Bakers, from Windbury. We run a pastry factory called Bakers For You.”

Carden’s spine jolted. He narrowed his eyes at Annie. His heart suddenly throbbed.

No wonder.

*Of course she found someone else.*

Carden clenched his fists beneath the desk and forced his voice calm. Annie’s teenage face as they frolicked about Windbury’s blooming meadows appeared out of nowhere. He quashed it down. The past was dead and gone.

“Don’t be a stranger,” Carden said and offered them his civil-service calling card. “I’m from Windbury too and miss the place. The air’s not quite the same here in Portsmouth.”

“Sure isn’t.” Roger chuckled and picked up Carden’s card. “Don’t know how you capital folk tolerate this foulness for a living.”

Roger’s face froze when he read Carden’s card. His eyes darted between the card and Carden’s face, and Carden merely responded with a playful smile. Roger finally passed Carden’s card to Annie.

“Carden!” Annie lunged across the desk and wrapped him in a crushing hug. His heart leapt to his throat. Not good!

“Surprise.” Carden patted Annie’s back with every scrap of restraint and nudged her off. Just the touch of their bodies ignited something fierce inside his ribs. He could not disrespect Annie and her husband.

“By the Father, Roger, we’re saved!” Annie turned to Roger and shook the man’s shoulders, a freneticism so at odds with her middle-aged bearing. She sure aged well.

Roger grinned, produced a file from his briefcase, and plopped it open in front of Carden. “We would owe you tremendously if you could help us.”

And so Roger and Annie described at length how their pastry factory diverted five-percent of their production output to feed Windbury’s urban poor. Carden reviewed the files just to be sure, it sounded too good to be true despite his fondness for Annie. Darn it, all the numbers looked sound. Annie and Roger even filed the philanthropy in their tax records for the past five years. A noble effort now devoured by the ten-percent corporate tax hike.

If only Annie and Roger had committed financial fraud, it would have made this so much easier.

“I wish I could help, but my hands are tied.” Carden winced and hoped his expression did show how genuinely he meant that. “Labor’s Cabinet will expand the army and navy no matter what. Government needs all the money it can get.”

Annie’s lip trembled. “People are losing their livelihoods, Carden.”

Carden averted his gaze from Annie’s glistening eyes and focused on the documents instead. Unfortunately, Roger pointed at several lines on one ledger. “The number of people coming to our soup-kitchen has doubled in three months, Carden.”

Carden almost blurted out it wasn’t his problem but held his tongue at the last moment. Annie deserved better.

“Why not set a quota then on how many can come by the soup kitchen? Everyone’s going through hard times right now, everywhere. People will understand.”

Annie crossed her arms and blinked at Carden. “Carden, tell me how you will look a hungry family of seven in the eye and make them understand, especially the children.”

Carden leaned on the desk and rubbed his temple. Why did fate or whichever god Annie and Roger worshipped engineer this blasted reunion? Of the nine Special Commissioners of Income Tax on this floor, Annie and Roger found him. What a bad joke.

“Carden, these aren’t strangers. Half our graduating class have lost their jobs in the past three months. It’s friends and family we’re talking about too.”

*Annie, please stop.*

Carden turned to Roger. He needed to confirm something. “Are you an Arcanist?”

Roger nodded.

“Come with me.”

Carden knocked on Edward’s door. When the man inside bade them in, Carden introduced Annie and Roger to his superior and then said, “I would like my fellow well-meaning citizens to understand why our society is making these current sacrifices, sir. They are important figures in Windbury’s business circles and it would help tremendously if they grasp the magnitude of what we are contributing to.”

Edward clapped his hands and beamed. “Of course! Please, we are a truly blessed generation, my dear fellows.”

Carden kept his guard up. Edward’s politician mask brooked no chink today. Hard to say if the man earnestly believed those words or not. Best Carden kept this conversation minimal.

Edward passed Roger the God of War’s recent *Scripture* and right away Roger wobbled on his feet. He nearly dropped the document before Edward deftly caught it from underneath. Edward said, “Quite something, isn’t it.”

“I feel truly awed, sir.” Roger braced himself on Annie’s arm. Carden looked away.

Back in Carden’s office, Carden locked the door and then asked in a much quieter tone, “You understand?”

Roger rubbed his right wrist and flashed for the briefest of moments a military-tattoo. A blue kingfisher that glided over blue water. Ah, the Fifth Marine Regiment, the Blue Kingfishers. Roger understood then.

“You know how bad this is, right, Carden?” Roger asked.

Carden kept his expression neutral. “Not a word to anyone you know until Parliament makes the announcement.”

Annie shrank in her seat. “Times were already tough before all this, and now the government is squeezing us for more. All to help the God of War burn the world down.”

Annie abruptly straightened. “But that’s beside the point. You still have the power to authorize tax exemptions, yes? We just need to give you compelling reasons.”

Carden bit his lip. “They would have to be exceptional circumstances, Annie. Matters that implicate national security or strategic interests.”

Annie got up and nudged Roger after her. “You never visit home, Carden. Why don’t you come by for the weekend? Deliverance Day is this Saturday. We can all celebrate together, including your parents, and you can see for yourself how important our work is to keep Windbury’s peace.”

Carden never visited home *because* of his parents. Either Annie forgot he suffered bad blood with his parents or she hoped to reconcile them. Knowing her, both possibilities seemed equally likely. Besides, Horace stepped up as Windbury’s mayor this Friday. A weekend visit just sounded like trouble.

“Please, Carden.”

By the Father. Annie’s expression dredged something up his throat. Did she remember his broken promise?

“I’ll come for the weekend and assess your case based on what I see.”

That was the best he could say short of the truth.

Later that night Carden cooked a stew of smoked sardines, pumpkin, potatoes, carrots, and sliced mushrooms at home. He served the stew alongside a platter of sliced rye-bread while Marelyn pored over today’s stock indexes. A lovely purse of rich indigo with gold buttons sat on the table’s corner.

Best they got straight to business.

“Come eat,” Carden said, dipped a slice of rye in the stew, and offered it to Marelyn. Marelyn snapped the loaf off his fingers, paused with eyes wide, and then swallowed the loaf with a sigh. She put down her papers and looked at Carden with a complicated expression.

“Something this sublime almost tastes like an apology.” Marelyn scooped a generous serving of stew with another slice of rye and scarfed it down. “I’m assuming you met Horace?”

“I told him to mind his business, yes.” Carden gobbled dinner and spoke between mouthfuls.

Marelyn rubbed the loaf between her fingers until crumbs trickled onto the table. “That’s all?”

Marelyn refused to meet his gaze. A tinge of dread wiggled into Carden’s gut. “Yes.”

“I see.”

The remainder of dinner passed in silence. Carden blamed Horace. When they finished the last loaf and Carden unstoppered a bottle of red wine for them, Marelyn asked, “What else happened at work today?”

Carden paused pouring Marelyn’s glass.

“I met Annie.”

Their flat’s silence blared.

“I see.”

Carden came around to Marelyn sunk in the leather couch with those inscrutable green eyes affixed to their living room’s featureless stucco ceiling. He slipped the wineglass into her stiff hand and sat across on the wooden rocking chair. Nothing like rhythmic motion to forget trouble.

“So how is your old flame doing?”

How Marelyn managed to utter such multilayered questions with such inaneness, Carden could never understand. He only knew in these moments Marelyn demanded the truth even as she herself skirted the real question. He quenched his throat and lubricated his thoughts with wine.

“She’s happily married to one Roger Baker and they both run a successful pastry factory back home. It took a lot to hold my composure talking to them.”

Marelyn scoffed, sipped her wine, and then paused. “Roger… black hair that looks brown under a certain light, fuzzy sideburns, deep brown eyes, a few centimeters shorter than you?”

Carden stopped the rocking chair. “That’s an impeccably accurate description of the man.”

Out of the blue Marelyn patted the spot next to her on the couch. Carden blinked at Marelyn until she glared, whereupon he scooted right over. Marelyn locked her arms around his arm and pressed her face against it as if clinging to a mast. Moments later his skin felt wet under the sleeve.

Oh.

Carden stroked Marelyn’s lap while she fought back sobs. Marelyn squeezed tighter.

“Why do we both need to live like this?”

“Why won’t things ever go my way?”

“Why can’t I be me?”

Carden slipped free of Marelyn’s grip, wrapped her up in his arms, and snuggled her against his chest. She wriggled into his space and sobbed while he patted her back. When Marelyn finally fell asleep Carden tucked her in bed, sat alone by the hearth, and stared at the last dying embers.

He asked himself those questions every single day.

How preposterous he only learned after twenty-five years Marelyn did too.

“You’re sure you don’t want to come with me back to Windbury?” Carden asked Saturday morning as he packed a briefcase with a weekend’s worth of clothes.

Marelyn shook her head and smiled with genuine warmth. “I’d rather not run into our parents. Or Roger and Annie. Just give them my regards.”

As Carden crossed their flat’s threshold, Marelyn’s warm fingers suddenly laced his.

“Safe travels. Get home soon.”

What a surreal start to the day that only promised more strangeness to come.

*Am I going mad or does the countryside actually look worse?*

The Portsmouth-Windbury train conveniently crossed the island’s entire southern coast. How could it be possible that three hours into this four-hour train journey every countryside Commune he passed looked worse for wear? Unrepaired damage on concrete palisades meant to protect Communes from Aetheric Beast and Arcane Beast depredations. Acres upon acres of continuous fallow fields rather than the typical field-rotations. Roads, power lines, and telegraph lines in disrepair.

Restless spirits of the dead wandering the span between Communes in broad daylight.

“What is our country coming to?”

A withered old woman in drab cotton overalls suddenly stopped next to Carden’s seat. “It’s been this way getting worse the past eight years.”

Carden blinked.

“Doesn’t matter if it’s Labor, Trade, Dominions, People, Freedom, or Justice squatting in Prudence Street. They’s don’t care about us living out here.”

Carden obliged the old granny with a smile until she lost interest and padded away. Carden pulled his notepad out and scribbled his observations. Edward needed to know about the state-of-affairs outside Portsmouth when Carden returned. At the very least everything he saw pointed to an imminent crisis-of-government.

Deliverance Day normally served as a synonym for festive holiday cheer, since it represented the day the Father sent His sole begotten Son to the world for the salvation of all Dwarves, Elves, and Humans. When Carden stepped out of Windbury Train Station, Windbury Cathedral across Central Square rose grey and glum. Bedraggled, shivering men wrapped in dirty cloaks and coats queued before the cathedral’s steps where lay clergy ran a tented soup-kitchen.

The horse-drawn omnibus ride to Annie and Roger’s factory showed worse. The carriage’s four horses all wheezed with visible ribs, his fellow passengers looked equal parts nasty and hungry, and the omnibus driver snapped whip and mouth in wild abandon. Every time Carden glanced out the carriage, refuse and human excrement littered curbs while stores looked sad or shuttered.

What in the Father’s name did Horace promise these folk to get elected? Whoever served as mayor before did a sodden job, it bordered on criminal.

“You’re finally here!” Annie’s face brightened and she hugged Carden without warning. Carden froze and flashed an amused Roger a nervous chuckle. Roger and Annie’s four teenage-children waved and then continued their work passing out pouches of hardtack to the poor. Insane these days the word poor encompassed people dressed as respectable clerks, concierges, and managers.

“We’re giving a little more hardtack since it’s Deliverance Day.” Annie ushered Carden behind the counter and showed the piles of pouched alms. “The Father would expect nothing less from us. We also gave Windbury Cathedral several batches of bread for mass tonight.”

“That’s very kind of you,” Carden said even as his eyes twitched at how the queue of needy wrapped around the factory block.

“So what do you think?” Roger asked while Annie took his place running the counter.

Carden avoided Annie and Roger’s children who all shared Annie’s bright eyes and Roger’s warm smile. No reason to dwell on what never happened. The present demanded him.

“I believe we can make a compelling argument that—”

“Down with government!”

Carden spotted the rock in the corner of his eye and ducked. It whistled past him and smacked Roger’s chest. Annie screamed.

“How dare you commit assault on a government official!” Carden lunged over the counter and flared his Arcanist powers. Sky-blue Magic blazed out him like flames and the crowd so incensed earlier scrambled away. The man who threw the rock earlier cowered with bent legs. Carden gripped him by the scruff of his patched coat and shook him back and forth.

“I am here to understand the plight of Windbury’s honest men and women.” Carden infused every word with Zeal Magic. “Why do you think I was in that soup-kitchen, you bloke?”

The onlookers shrank further back. Carden’s target whimpered. He tossed the man towards the soup-kitchen where Roger staggered to his feet with Annie’s help. Glad Roger so far looked fine if a touch shaken.

“You will apologize to the Bakers, now.”

Carden paused as a familiar Aetheric sensation tickled his mystical senses. Someone here was using Zeal Magic. A fellow Ideals Arcanist. Carden met the woman’s almond-shaped eyes right as she slunk out the crowd and strutted right up to him. Bright scarves draped over a fine burgundy wool trench-coat and long auburn hair tied back to reveal pointed ears. What was a moneyed family’s blue-blooded daughter doing here?

“And why must he apologize?” the woman asked and pointed at Roger. “You ducked. Typical of government to let the innocent suffer the consequences.”

“That’s a dangerous game,” Carden said and loomed over the shorter woman. Unfortunately, she held her ground and even repulsed Carden’s Zeal Magic with her own. Actually, the crowd looked less mollified by the second. Darn it, an Idealist Arcanist at least the same Tier as him.

“Are you implying, madam, that hard times condone open assault on your fellows?” Carden spanned his arms outwards and turned in a circle to face the crowd’s gaze. “I was born and raised in Windbury. This is my home! Do you think it doesn’t hurt me to see what’s happening?”

He just needed to placate the crowd and smother this tinderbox before it exploded. Carden whirled on the half-Elf woman and growled in Arcane Tongue for her ears alone. The irrefutable medium for truth between Arcanists.

“I am Carden Oakmoore, Special Commissioner of Income Taxes with the Customs and Revenue Board. The Bakers asked me to help them secure a tax-exemption. They invited me here to show their good work for you and your neighbors.”

The young woman’s eyes widened and Carden jabbed a finger at her shoulder. “So I suggest you get back in line and retract your Magic before—”

His feet felt the explosion before his ears. Carden yelled for the crowd to get down and leapt behind a parked horse-carriage when the muffled shockwave harmlessly passed. A distant plume of smoke rose from behind the street’s warehouses. Closer yells and screams, some in pain and many in anger, roared above the din.

Carden’s rapid-fire thoughts condensed into ice-cold dread.

Carden whirled on the suspected instigator and ringleader, except she already vanished. The crowd rapidly dispersed with no figure in bright scarves among them.

“Damn it!”

Distant Aetheric fluctuations rippled the air a moment later. Carden exchanged a look with Roger, the man nodded grimly, and that conveyed everything that needed to be said. Roger herded his family into their factory. Carden pulled out his commissioner’s whistle and blew.

“Any rioter violating the peace will be shot!”

The Father truly turned His face away from Carden and his home. Carden flared his sky-blue Magical aura into a billowing blaze. To think the day came he needed to bring his military specialty down on his own home.

More muffled explosions and plumes of smoke rose, accompanied by the din of riotous, arguably righteous anger.

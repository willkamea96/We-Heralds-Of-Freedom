# Chapter One – Our Definitions Of Freedom


“Hail to thee, Heralds of Freedom!”

The Dwarf man’s baritone voice echoed about the spacious Parliament floor and drew its equivalent response from his Human counterparts.

“Hail to thee, Pillars of Iron!”

Dwarves and Humans of all genders bowed to one another and finished their people’s exchange of ancient mantras. Dwarves in their homeland’s beige cloaks and caps, Humans in local business suits and high-cut dresses. The rote steps for the historic compact between Mankind, the Heralds of Freedom, and Dwarfkind, the Pillars of Iron, that always preceded this most dreary and mundane of recurrent rituals.

Carden stifled a yawn—and almost yelped because his wife Marelyn elbowed him. Marelyn’s glare smothered the words on his tongue. Carden frowned and suffered the subsequent proceedings in somber stiffness. If only his wife gave that much regard for their marital life.

Below them, all but two of the Dwarf and Human delegates took their seats on opposite sides of Alfainia’s Parliament Chamber. The Dwarf delegate, Feroz Surena, met his Human counterpart, Alfainia’s newly-elected Prime-Minister, at the center of the chamber’s scuffed marble floor where Feroz announced in booming Arcane Tongue.

“Greetings, honored Human. Do you speak on behalf of Alfainia’s people?”

Carden rolled his eyes. If only that sorry man Alfainia elected as its newest Prime-Minister stopped readjusting his business tie. Damned shameful tic for someone about to run the country. When that man finally cracked a bow, it unfortunately stretched his suit a tad too taut. Carden winced as the greasy-haired man droned back in Arcane Tongue.

“Honored Dwarf, by the evidence of this year’s election do I, Terence Howell, speak on behalf of Alfainia.”

Around the room, pens scratched notepads like rats between walls. Poor choice of words in front of all these journalists. This Prime-Minister already cobbled together the most bloated parliamentary coalition in recent history for his narrow win, if only he understood that meant no room for personal weakness. Hopefully he accomplished something, anything.

Ideally not catastrophic, if the Father so graced.

Carden leaned into Marelyn’s ear. “I would love to hear your family’s thoughts on our new cabinet.”

Marelyn bit her lips, tucked a loose lock of her short strawberry-blonde hair behind her ears, and said nothing. Not that it mattered, they both voted for different parties this election cycle. Just one of their many, proliferous disagreements. Shame since neither of their picks made it into the ruling coalition.

Feroz Surena combed his frizzy chestnut hair back with both hands and beckoned the new Prime-Minister, Terence Howell, to kneel. If it bothered the Dwarf that even with Terence down on one knee the Human still loomed over him, Feroz’s flat countenance betrayed nothing. At least this latest Dwarf dignitary wore thicker skin than his predecessor.

“Then by the authority our God of War has invested in me as His Lord Magi over Alfainia,” Feroz Surena clapped his hands once and an ephemeral sword aglow with dusk’s radiance blazed to life atop them, “do I, Feroz of House Surena, acknowledge thee, Terence Howell, as Alfainia’s Prime-Minister.”

Right as Feroz waved his sword overhead, a terrible force slammed Carden down. His bones rattled as if they begged to supplicate. Everyone around him, from the audience above to the dignitaries below, stirred. Dwarves slid off their seats and prostrated. Humans dropped on their knees. Carden steadied Marelyn’s trembling hand in his and she offered no resistance when he led them down to their knees.

Something went terribly wrong. The investiture ritual’s rules explicitly forbade any expression of divinity by any party. Feroz broke the rules, yet why did Alfainia’s Demigods do nothing?

Quick as a ghost the pressure vanished. A knot in Carden’s gut unclenched, he sucked a mouthful of air, and Marelyn’s hand in his relaxed. Sighs of relief broke the silence. Carden glanced at the balconies where Alfainia’s Demigods resided—and found pale-faced men and women staggering to their feet.

One Demigod, no matter how strong, could not simultaneously restrain so many of his peers with such a brief outburst. Not in the many times Carden witnessed Demigod duels on the mainland. That earlier crush of divinity was not Feroz’s, then.

Oh no.

Feroz lifted his arms and stared reverently at the chamber’s marble-domed ceiling, magnificently frescoed with the God of War’s silhouette against sunset. ”We hail thee, God of War!”

“Hail!” Feroz’s fellow Dwarves chanted.

The most bloated, pathetic ruling coalition in recent history honored by the God of War’s uninvited personal presence. What preposterous implications it left in so brief a passing. Hopefully it meant Carden still had a job in the civil service.

Feroz tapped Terence’s shoulders with his orange Magical sword. “Be honored, Terence Howell. The God of War Himself acknowledges your election as Prime-Minister. May you and your government continue to treasure the great friendship between Alfainia and Sarh.”

Sarh’s priest-cum-diplomats never failed to deliver that subtle reminder, except today those words stabbed Carden down to the bone. For the first time since the first investiture ritual however many decades ago, the God of War’s presence manifested. Did the God of War want Alfainia to remember who it owed its liberation to seventy-four-years ago?

The rest of Terence’s new ruling cabinet, from Finance Minister to Defense Minister to Religious Affairs Minister, knelt beside Terence. Every man and woman bowed their heads while Feroz’s sunset-orange sword consecrated them with brief, resplendent sunset-orange halos.

Feroz beckoned Alfainia’s newest ruling government to their feet, whereupon the Humans clasped their right fists across their hearts in the God of War’s salute. Terence and his lackeys all chanted in Arcane Tongue, that mystically inviolable language only those born with the Arcane’s Magic could understand. Another deviation from the regular ritual. Everyday vernacular sufficed for this part. The God of War’s gaze threw every prescribed rite out the door.

“Thus do we, the leaders of Alfainia, pledge our allegiance and faith to the God of War!”

Beside Carden, Marelyn sniffed.

“Traitors.”

Marelyn’s underhanded comment drew a few pointed looks from their neighbors. Carden placed a hand on the curve of Marelyn’s cold back. It took everything to restrain himself to a simple clench of the jaw.

“What was that, my dear? Something about afternoon tea?” Carden asked in an appropriately louder voice and hoped it sounded inane enough to those nosy onlookers.

Marelyn scoffed and leaned on Carden, their long-agreed-upon modicum of intimacy for the public eye. She felt stiff as a board and cold as a stone. Marelyn said in the blandest of tones, “Can’t believe my family skipped this for afternoon tea.”

At least the lie worked. People around them turned back to the ceremony below. Marelyn rested more of her weight on Carden and then whispered under her breath.

“We used to believe in something. We used to believe in the Father. This is who we’re electing now.”

Not this conversation again. Carden slid his hand around Marelyn’s waist and pressed her close. Marelyn looked up at him as if hungry for his answer, but he offered none. She already knew his opinion on the matter, no reason he needed to indulge her self-righteousness here in so public a forum. Her disappointment at his silence felt heavy in his arm. Marelyn spoke nothing more as the investiture ritual closed on familiar applause by Parliament’s representatives and Alfainia’s civil servants.

Yet the God of War’s divine presence reverberated long after. Every person Carden and Marelyn met during post-ceremony refreshments gushed endlessly about that honor. The more flattery Carden suffered the worse the reception’s buttered potatoes, braised veal, and champagne tasted. And of course Marelyn’s frustration at the drivel expressed itself in how painfully she clenched Carden’s hand. His poor hand did not deserve this abuse on behalf of all these sycophants.

“Enough.” Carden tugged Marelyn to a balcony that overlooked the busy and grey Rorsch River. He shut the glass door behind them with all the gentleness left in him, plastered a smile for the crowd behind the glass, but edged his words in iron.

“Would you prefer we leave?”

Marelyn turned her back to the building and her green eyes drank in the turbulent river’s bustling activity. The silence she dragged out fanned Carden’s face hotter. Why did she always make things so complicated?

“You served several combat-tours on the mainland before we married. You couldn’t have lasted that long if you didn’t believe in what our country fought for. So why didn’t you say anything back there, Carden?”

Blackened, artillery-blasted fields from horizon to horizon replaced the urban sprawl below Carden. Burnt flesh, steaming blood, and sweet propellant-smoke tickled his nose. Carden shook his head and clasped his hands together before the jitters worsened.

“A good soldier follows orders.”

Finally, Marelyn turned her piercing gaze on him. Her cheek twitched and her eyes shot such vehemence it tingled Carden’s spine.

“So that’s it, end of story? The distinguished veteran rewarded for his service by being appointed Special Commissioner for Income Tax shrugs off a betrayal of founding principles on the argument he is following orders?”

Carden clenched his teeth and forced his smile to stay up. Too many nosy people nearby, right behind the windows and glass door.

“If you would remain happy being my wife without my present salary and civil service benefits, I would be more than grateful to resign.”

Nothing but the capital’s smog-tainted wind whistled in response.

“I think I’m ready to leave, Carden.”

Carden never felt more relieved today until he finally closed the carriage-door behind Marelyn and asked the chauffer to take her home. Carden lit a cigar and huffed this precious moment of peace alone by the crowded curb. Finally, some semblance of the jolly good time everyone in the Parliament Building behind him seemed to so easily find.

Simple pleasures.

“We have been given a mandate by the God of War,” Alfainia’s new Finance Minister said from behind his desk when Carden answered the man’s summons to the minister’s office in the Commonwealth Exchequer. A cubical concrete space of bare pinewood furnishings, starkly divided in half into light and dark by how the sun glared through one tall window. Insufferable brightness by the minister’s desk, deep shadow by the door where Carden stood.

Edward Cook, the new Finance Minister and head of the Trade Party, tossed his suit onto the coat-stand right beside Carden with unerring accuracy. That casualness did not look practiced. Carden inhaled and took his new superior’s measure. Picture-perfect golden-blonde hair combed back, full groomed beard, and sea-blue eyes that bored into Carden’s.

Most importantly, this man’s presence slightly stirred the air’s ever-pervasive, invisible Aether. A fellow practitioner of the Arcane’s Magic then.

“I understand you are a veteran of both civil service and military service, Mister Oakmoore?”

Carden stood at attention. “Yes, sir.”

“Which unit?”

The faces of long-gone comrades flickered over Edward’s head. Carden swallowed. “Three-hundred-and-ninth Infantry Regiment, sir. Eighth Infantry Division.”

Edward froze, eyes narrowed. The man muttered after an odd pause. “The Black Hounds. So you’re an Arcanist from that regiment, interesting. Your files omitted that.”

How kind of the people down in Personnel.

“I specialized in pacifying occupied enemy settlements, sir.” Carden forced his voice into the flattest tone possible. He needed to set the right expectations with Edward so the next four years working under the man proved at least tolerable. “Perhaps the civil service did not want that little detail parroted, since I do set people’s taxes.”

Edward grimaced. “And here I pigeonholed my predecessors as utter fools. Guess even broken clocks chime right twice a day.”

No need to mention Carden felt the exact same way about Edward prior to this meeting.

“Quite right, sir.”

Carden sat across Edward when beckoned and picked up the stiff document Edward slid across. From a touch Carden knew right away who wrote it. A trace of divinity seeped like liquid fire into his soul while a disembodied male voice rumbled in Arcane Tongue somewhere high above.

“*The board is almost set.** The final pieces are moving**. **The time rapidly approaches when **the world war** our peoples long awaited will be unleashed**.*”

Carden’s heart clenched like a rock lodged in his chest.

“*People of Alfainia, prepare. **Raise your armies**, c**ommission your fleets. **The dawn when we topple the Creator **and his empire **comes.*”

Carden set the letter down—no, the God of War’s *Scripture*, and hoped Edward missed how Carden’s hand tremored. Arcane Tongue only allowed one to speak and write the truth. So the Arcane itself legitimized the *Scripture’s* authenticity, notwithstanding he just heard the God of War’s actual voice transcribed into the document.

Another world war.

Memories of vast armadas swam through Carden’s mind. Squadrons of ironclad dreadnoughts nearby bombarded a sprawling coastal city while Carden and his unit hopped into bobbing landing crafts. Near-misses and screams of the dying wrenched Carden’s heart as he stormed through burning streets and machine-gun fire. Up ahead entrenched behind rubble, rows of the Creator’s Imperial Legionaries in their bulletproof full-bodied armor awaited with assault-rifles ready. High above, Demigods and Angels dueled in displays that warped and cracked the sky.

“This cabinet was elected on the platform of improving the working class’s socioeconomic conditions,” Carden said although how he strung the words together his mind could not fathom. It sounded stupid but he dearly clung on to something, anything, that promised relief from wretched memories. The world already suffered too much. His eyelid twitched. “I don’t see how we can expand the military for a world war without drastic fiscal changes. How will the people react? Is there even a timeline for all this?”

Of course the people’s opinion mattered nothing and of course no timeline existed. Gods, whether the Creator, the Father, or the God of War, loved to dictate in ambiguities. Carden just needed to keep the conversation going until he regained his bearings. 

Edward grinned like a wolf. “That’s where you come in, Special Commissioner of Income Taxes.”

*Oh no*.

“We’ll let Terence and his Labor Party take care of the public relations,” Edward droned on, oblivious it seemed to Carden’s stiff posture. “I certainly don’t envy him the task.”

Poor Terence. Not even a day as Prime-Minister and already cornered into breaking his principle campaign promise. Carden prayed the Father looked kindly upon Terence for his trials. The Father knew Carden himself needed all the help Heaven could spare.

“Whereas we here in Customs and Revenue will scrape the barrel to the nibs.” Edward clapped the desk and pushed himself off. “I will be forwarding Parliament a corporate tax-hike in the coming days. Expect to be busy, Mister Oakmoore. I’m sure there will be many entitled entrepreneurs knocking on your door begging exemptions and relief. You will turn them all down, I trust?”

Carden suppressed the bitter laughter that itched his throat. “Yes, sir.”

Edward patted Carden’s shoulder. “Good man. That will be all.”

“Dismissed.”

Carden’s guilty pleasure of fine-dining at Wheatfield’s Bistro did nothing that night. Vintage wine tasted dry, caviar and sea-grapes lacked their characteristic succulence, and even Chef Wheatfield’s special salad of creamed squashes scraped his tongue. No culinary indulgence overcame the dread of working four long years under Edward Cook.

If the God of War’s engineered world war did not mercifully liberate Carden from life first.

Marelyn dozed over the dining table when Carden got back to his dark flat near midnight. Financial documents splayed across the wood in place of food. The air’s chill bit his skin while the living room’s hearth sat cold and dead. Carden sighed, hung his coat, and snuck into the kitchen. He preheated a steamer-pot over the stove, basked in its warmth, and once his fingers grew limber threw on an apron and got to work.

Luckily he restocked the pantry two days ago. Carden mashed together a marinade of garlic, peppercorn, and olive oil, and then dumped canned, brined duck into the paste. As that lathered duck steamed in the steamer-pot, he sliced a baguette loaf into a dozen slices and diced together a bruschetta paste of Arlinbury tomatoes, onions, and basil. He finished up right as the steamer-pot’s savory aroma rekindled his hunger. Good, enough water left in the pot. Carden added another steamer-tray above the duck and rehydrated the sliced baguette with herb-laced steam.

Carden returned to the dining room right as Marelyn stirred awake. He cleared the documents in front of her and laid the platter of duck bruschetta right under her nose. Marelyn blinked at the food as if presented a riddle and her lips moved yet uttered nothing.

“If this is a dream it’s a very strange one,” Marelyn finally said and rubbed her chest.

Carden removed his apron and snatched a bite. This tasted a lot better than his earlier dinner at Wheatfield’s Bistro. Good blend of juicy and crispy with just the right tartness and savoriness.

“You seem to forget we’re married.” Carden gestured at the documents Marelyn worked on to the point of sleep. “Just as you forgot to eat.”

“We both know where my talents lie,” Marelyn said and for the first time today cracked a smile.

Carden chuckled and asked, “So, how are our investments?”

Marelyn quirked an eyebrow at him as she devoured slice after slice.

“You’re hiding from something.”

Carden winced. How impeccable his parents arranged him to marry such an astute woman. Made it so difficult sometimes to just pretend he and Marelyn could enjoy a normal, warm domestic moment. He cleared his throat and started.

“The God of War had ulterior motives. Government just received a *Scripture* commanding Alfainia to prepare for a world war.”

“I see.” Marelyn dabbed her mouth with a handkerchief. “So Labor is already breaking its campaign promise and you will enforce their higher taxes.”

Carden nodded, grabbed a pitcher and two cups, and poured water for them both.

“Well, to answer your earlier question, you just provided priceless insider knowledge. It looks like I can optimize our portfolio a lot more.” Marelyn opened her notebook, pen in hand, and then for some odd reason hesitated before closing it. “No, that can wait till tomorrow. What do you think about all this?”

Marelyn’s green eyes sparkled at Carden.

Not this again. Guess Carden couldn’t avoid Marelyn’s earlier question anymore. If only she were a little less bright and a little more forgetful. Then again, if the next four years of his career looked grim, he might as well patch his marital life.

“Hypocritical. The God of War and his lackeys in Parliament are dangling the illusion of choice but dictate the options. Our country is just being used as a forward-operating base and self-funding arsenal into the Nordic continent. Doesn’t matter Alfainia gets poorer if it means the God of War conquers the Creator’s remaining territory on the mainland.”

Carden and Marelyn sat in companiable silence after. Not sure what sparked the embers, but for the first time in a long time this arranged marriage felt… right.

Guess Marelyn felt the same way because she rubbed her golden wedding-ring and its fat diamond. “Why can’t we make this work, Carden?”

Carden stared at his finger’s plain gold wedding band. That ever-present shackle worn for the past twenty-five years that he and Marelyn’s families insisted they call joyous matrimony.

“Because our parents never gave us a choice.”

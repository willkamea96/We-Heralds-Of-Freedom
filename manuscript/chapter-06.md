# Chapter Six – Messy Implementations Of Righteousness


“My hands are tied, Annie, Roger, please.”

Carden made a helpless gesture with both hands. He shut his eyes a moment later because of the looks Annie and Roger shot him across the office-desk.

“Carden, you have to help us, please!” Annie’s voice denied him respite. “Have you been back to Windbury at all lately? So many more people are without jobs now, we run the soup-kitchen all day!”

Perhaps Carden needed to take this as his cue to resign. The Father in Heaven knew he already enjoyed a good run in the civil-service. No one could fault him for doing his best this whole time under the circumstances.

“This is not just about your enterprise,” Carden said and hoped his tone came off grave enough. “You are asking me to give you more tax-exemptions *and* grant equivalent tax-exemptions to five of our other friends. Five.”

“Be thankful it’s only five, Carden!” Annie snapped and her cheeks flushed. “Do you realize how hard it is these days to get people to help others?”

*Can you not extend that same consideration to me**, then**?*

“I’m sorry, Annie, truly,” Carden said and then picked up the file Annie and Roger brought earlier. “But I’m already under intense scrutiny with government bleeding so much money to rearmament.”

Annie looked ready to add more but Roger raised a hand and asked first, “Are things really that bad?”

Carden pulled out a folder from his desk-drawer and slid it across to Roger. “Not a word of what’s in there leaves this room.”

Roger’s mouth dropped open halfway through his reading, Roger’s trembling fingers over Charles Winston’s signature at the folder’s cover.

“If you must trouble someone over why we need all these taxes, do lodge your displeasure with your very own local elected member of Parliament.” Carden took the folder back. Roger’s face looked ready to strangle someone. Carden said before it came to that, “Your efforts are better served directed at the new government budget’s strongest advocate rather than its unfortunate implementer.”

“I most certainly will when I get back.” Roger exhaled and Annie rubbed his shoulder with a look of concern. “Does Horace Livingstone know?”

“I would be surprised if he didn’t,” Carden said and paused. The few times Carden ran into Horace these past few months in Portsmouth, the grimmer the man’s demeanor. Quite clear that the burden of maintaining law-and-order in a city impoverished by taxes took a heavy toll on the new mayor. “What he can do about it, though, I’m not sure.”

“But you can still grant exemptions, yes?” Roger asked with an edge to his voice.

Carden leveled Roger an unamused look. “If I have given up on my job security and if doing so preempts the need for drastic military response to catastrophic riots, then yes.”

“Carden!” Annie slammed the table.

Carden winced. He probably should not have uttered that last part. Damn it. If only this conversation didn’t grate his patience down to that point.

“Then you should, Carden,” Roger said flatly. “It would mean the difference between getting by and starving for tens of thousands of people back home.”

Carden flicked through Annie and Roger’s submitted philanthropic proposal again in the vain hope maybe a fourth read finally rewarded some hitherto elusive insight. Unfortunately none came. Bakers For you and five other enterprises run by Carden’s old classmates partnered together to launch a citywide welfare program in Windbury. Noble by any measure, but desperately strapped for free cash flow because a sizeable chunk went to the latest taxes.

So much to digest in one unplanned sitting that Carden’s mind ached.

“I’ll see what I can do.” Carden closed the offending folder and folded his hands across his belly. A belly thinner by the month as his working hours grew longer and thus deprived him of opportunities to indulge guilty pleasures at Wheatfield’s Bistro. Not that Marelyn complained about his waistline, quite the opposite.

Annie abruptly reached across the desk and clasped Carden’s hands in her cold ones. Carden shot her a quizzical look and then noticed Roger’s impassive expression from the corner of his eye. If only Carden could help.

“Please, Carden,” Annie said and her almond-shaped eyes seemed to grow larger. “For old time’s sake.”

Carden pried Annie’s hands off as gently as he could. “No promises.”

After Carden accompanied Annie and Roger to the stairs, he stalked to Edward’s office with the philanthropic file in hand. He never even made it to the door.

“How dare you grant blanket exemptions to all these enterprises in Aberclair!”

Carden froze half-step. Edward’s voice could have rattled the concrete walls.

“And you! How will I explain to Parliament that Pontmyr’s tax revenues fell short of target?”

A good soldier picked his battles. Carden scurried back to his office and swore he would try again tomorrow. Unfortunately, as soon as he plopped in his seat and reached for a cigar, a well-dressed petitioner knocked. The man entered before Carden even gave permission and plopped in the seat across Carden, desperation plainly written across his creased face.

This was going to be a long day.

Carden came home with Annie and Roger’s file in his briefcase. Upon second thought earlier as he closed the office, maybe the Father blessed Carden by making Edward indisposed. It made more sense to discuss the case with Marelyn first. If Marelyn concurred this represented a horrible idea, no point in him risking Edward’s ire. Besides, Carden did promise Marelyn after all to face the future together. No better dilemma.

Strange, Marelyn was still out. Carden plopped the file on their dinner table and tidied up. He took a quick hot shower—a recent renovation for all civil-servant residences Carden still could not pinpoint how Parliament funded. Marelyn still hadn’t come home when Carden dried up, truly strange. Carden dove into the kitchen then and threw together a quick dinner. If Marelyn came home much later than Carden’s already late-shift, she probably worked up a fearsome appetite.

The mutton curry smelled ready when the front-door creaked open and Marelyn shuffled in. From how her steps dragged, conversation sounded like the last thing on her mind. Carden left her to her own devices. He mixed minced parsley and oregano into the curry, slathered one last layer of butter over the oven’s baking potatoes, and scratched a quick sauté of caramelized onions to go with the potatoes.

Carden brought dinner out to a Marelyn deeply engrossed in Annie and Roger’s file. So engrossed in fact she hadn’t changed from the beige collared blouse and brown long-hemmed dress she typically wore to the Stock Exchange.

“Come eat.” Carden laid the platters in front of her. “As much as we both love bringing work home, it’s not an excuse to skip dinner.”

Marelyn laughed without peeling her eyes away from Annie and Roger’s file. “Sorry, I got dinner earlier with… wait, is that mutton?”

Marelyn’s eyes swiveled to the stew and lingered on it with palpable intensity.

“I suppose a bite wouldn’t hurt.”

Many bites and two empty platters later, Carden sipped some red wine while Marelyn slouched across the dining table. Bloated in more ways than just food apparently.

Carden helped her to the washroom and prepared her nightgown on the rack outside while she showered. She came out, towel wrapped around her collarbone and cheeks aglow, and plopped on the couch with a sigh without so much as a glance at her prepared nightgown. Guess she really had a long day. Carden came over and massaged Marelyn’s bare shoulders. She tensed at first but then moaned for the rest of his ministrations.

“You probably guessed it,” Carden finally said after he was sure he already loosened the knots in Marelyn’s shoulders, “but Annie and Roger visited me at work earlier. Tough case.”

Marelyn exhaled. “How curious. I ran into Roger earlier at the Stock Exchange as it closed. Except, seeing how he sought you out first, I’m starting to think it wasn’t a simple coincidence.”

Carden laughed. “Did he treat you to a lovely dinner?”

Marelyn laughed too, reached back, and clasped one of Carden’s hands on her shoulder. “He did actually. He said Annie was off on some errand. I swear I half-worried he intended some type of advance on me. You can’t imagine my relief when he begged me to appeal to your compassion for our hometown.”

Carden paused.

“Marelyn… earlier at my office, I did get the impression Annie was being unusually forward.”

A heavy silence dragged out between them. Marelyn broke the silence first.

“They’re really desperate for our help, are they?” 

Carden started massaging Marelyn’s back, between the shoulder-blades and spine and down in a line to her pelvis. Marelyn groaned but otherwise said nothing.

“I’m honestly lost, Marelyn. I want to help, but this is such a sensitive time in Government. Doesn’t help something’s nagging me about the numbers in their proposal.”

Marelyn leaned on her knees when Carden moved to a particularly tight tendon above her pelvis.

“How wise of you to bring the matter to me then,” Marelyn said half-chuckling, half-groaning.

Carden didn’t need to divulge his first instinct earlier was to bring it straight to Edward.

“From what I could read earlier,” Marelyn said, “Annie and Roger’s numbers look perfectly legitimate to me. If you’re worried about financial irregularities or vulnerabilities for corporate malintent, I happen to know the third-party auditor Annie and Roger contracted for the philanthropy. That firm’s widely accredited. Annie, Roger, and your participating friends clearly did their research for public trust.”

Carden leaned down and pressed both thumbs against the curve of Marelyn’s back. Carden said while Marelyn groaned, “This makes things even more complicated.”

“Then just do the right thing. Sign the exemption forms, drop them off at Edward’s desk, see what happens.”

Carden’s fingers slipped off Marelyn’s back as her words finally sank in.

“That’s it?”

Marelyn craned her neck around and smirked at Carden.

“Why must you always make things so complicated?”

She made a good point.

The morning after Carden signed the exemption forms and dropped them off on Edward’s desk. As simple as Marelyn made it sound, anxiety ate away at Carden’s stomach all day. He half-expected every tap on his door to presage Edward’s furious entrance. Instead, petitioners came in one after another, he assessed their cases with one ear peeled for a slammed door, yet nothing happened.

And then the next morning, Carden came into the office and found the five exemption forms returned to his desk. Edward’s signature of approval on all of them in thick black strokes.

*It can’t be that simple, can it?*

Carden reviewed the forms in case he missed some annotation or remark of Edward’s, yet nothing denied the approvals. Carden leaned back against his seat and stared at the ceiling. He got up, half a mind to stop by Edward’s office, and paused. Marelyn would have told him to count his blessings and move on.

“I guess that’s it,” Carden said to himself and straightened when the first petitioner came.

Annie and Roger insisted on treating Carden and Marelyn to a nice dinner at Wheatfield’s Bistro, which Carden and Marelyn profusely refused. The work was reward enough. So Annie and Roger brought Carden and Marelyn across the Rorsch River to Northern Portsmouth’s Saint Alfred’s Cathedral at dusk to give thanks together.

“I can’t remember the last time I was here,” Carden said when they approached Saint Alfred’s statue before the yawning double-doors. Bearded, gentle-faced Saint Alfred clutched a *Scripture* from the Father in one hand and a bowl for alms in another, bathed in sunset.

“You’ve gone completely to the God of War?” Annie asked in a flat tone.

The promise Carden and Annie made to one another as teenagers back in Windbury Cathedral wafted back to Carden’s ears. How they vowed before the father to marry once Carden returned from the army. Carden shook the thought away.

“My faith hasn’t changed,” Carden said and laid a hand on the statue’s dusk-illuminated plaque, “just perhaps not as wholehearted as what the Father would like.”

Marelyn came over and rested her hand atop Carden’s on the statue’s plaque. “New beginnings then.”

Carden met Marelyn’s glistening eyes and agreed. “New beginnings.”

When the four seated themselves in the near-empty cathedral’s frontmost pew, Annie and Roger gave a prayer of thanks to the Father for His provision in their endeavor. And then without warning both rose and laid hands on Carden and Marelyn’s foreheads.

Roger prayed something for Marelyn that Carden could barely discern, because Annie’s prayer for him struck to the bone.

“Father in Heaven, Son above, I beseech Thee for Thy peace in Carden’s life. Free of the shackles from his parents, from his wars, and from his hate.”

Carden blinked at the empty space between him and the vacant sanctuary. By the time he came to, Annie and Roger already vanished. Carden glanced to his side and found Marelyn seated similarly still, a few tears on her cheeks.

He scooted over and cupped Marelyn’s hands in his, their wedding rings pressed together.

Marelyn said after a long moment’s reflection, “Let’s be happy, Carden.”

Carden squeezed Marelyn’s hands and uttered a quick prayer under the Father’s icon above them. The simple white marble cross, mounted across the stained window depicting the Son’s worldly life, gleamed before his eyes.

“Father in Heaven, Son above, Marelyn and I are ready to be happy.”

Months went by, the capital grew tenser as Alfainia’s Dominions reported ever more Imperial incursions, and life went on for Carden and Marelyn. Whether that prayer did nothing or invited all these problems, Carden preferred to keep it out of mind. Carden’s workload crept higher and higher, Marelyn’s choices for worthwhile stocks shrank, and they both commiserated over simple dinners at home.

At last summer’s sweltering heat flew away and with it Alfainia’s green leaves. Fall swept in with a premature chill. Alfainia celebrated the changing, falling leaves with the maiden voyage of the first fruits of its rearmament started a year ago. A grand festivity in Portsmouth Habor on one bright sunny day with people from far and wide in attendance.

“So this is where all our money went,” Marelyn said next to Carden, hand-in-hand, as they watched the aerial military parade overhead from the stands reserved for Parliament.

A few of their esteemed neighbors swiveled their heads to Marelyn and glared.

“Well, Government’s got to show something for all its efforts,” Carden said without missing stride. “Let our fellow citizens see this is the fruit of their labors.”

Some of said neighbors rolled their eyes while others scoffed. The next fly-by soared past and its droning, buzzing, and rumbling drowned their comments out. Aerial destroyers, frigates, and light-cruisers glided overhead and blotted out the sun. Squadron-after-squadron of turboprop fighter-planes, bomber-planes, and transport-planes zoomed between them. They all proudly emblazoned Alfainia’s colors and emblems on their polished hulls and fuselages. Out in Portsmouth Bay, Aflainia’s newest naval flotilla matched the aerial enthusiasm with blaring ship-horns and celebratory naval gunfire of all calibers.

Carden peered down the harbor-front towards the berths and promenades opened for the public’s attendance. A good number of young men and teenagers hooted, cheered, and sang Alfainia’s national anthem in discordant notes from the water’s edge. That small minority aside, the rest watched on with a noticeable sobriety and weariness. If Parliament hoped this little show stoked public enthusiasm, whoever approved this exorbitant expense on taxpayer money deserved a long meeting with the budgetary committee.

In the middle of autumn, with only a handful of fluttering orange leaves left on Portsmouth’s trees, Carden returned from lunch and found a familiar face in his office. Horace Livingstone, clad in his favorite sharp white suit, lounged in a chair. He twirled an unlit cigar in one hand and skimmed a document with the other. When Carden entered, Horace offered a smile that didn’t reach his eyes.

“Horace, it’s been awhile,” Carden said and went straight to the refreshments stand. “Water or cider?”

“Water’s fine, old friend,” Horace said, set his things down on Carden’s desk, and rose with a groan. “I won’t be long.”

“What brings you over?” Carden asked as he poured water from a decanter into two clear glasses.

Horace blinked at the water and then said, “Apologies, I suppose.”

Carden paused until water overflowed the glass he was pouring. Carden swore, wiped the spill with a rag, and offered the other glass to Horace. It took everything for his trembling hand not to spill the glass.

“I beg your pardon?”

“I had a meeting with Edward yesterday,” Horace said and his eyes remained glued to Carden’s chest, “to discuss Windbury’s municipal budget. It’s no secret the budget’s not where it needs to be what with so many folk out of jobs and not paying taxes. We haven’t even gotten around to all the property damage from last year’s riots. So I hoped Edward, since we had a good relationship and all, could extend us some funding from central.”

Carden’s blood curdled. Horace’s gaze finally met Carden’s and those eyes brooked no relief.

“Edward ran an audit on Windbury’s finances. He’s furious with your tax-exemptions.”

Horace closed the distance and clasped Carden’s shoulder in a firm grip. “I’m assuming the Bakers came to you begging for help with their charity program. You should have come to me, Carden. At least then I could have been careful how I approached Edward.”

Windbury Square lined in black shrouds came to mind. He should have, despite that. Carden and Horace went so far back. Why did it never occur to him to notify Horace, when he had all this time?

Horace patted Carden’s shoulder one last time and turned to the door.

“May the Father be with you, old friend.”

Not long after the door clicked shut, a knock came. Edward’s secretary appeared in the doorway with a perfectly neutral expression. No time to even get his bearings.

“Mister Oakmoore. Mister Cook would like to see you.”

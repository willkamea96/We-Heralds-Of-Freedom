# Industry Standards: Upmarket Adult Character-Driven Fantasy Novella

Reference compiled October 2026 for *We Heralds of Freedom* (Draft Zero, ~30,161 words).

---

## 1. Word Count and Format Definitions

### Novella

| Organization | Word-Count Range | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| SFWA / Nebula Awards | 17,500–39,999 words | The governing definition for U.S. speculative-fiction publishing |
| Hugo Awards (WSFS) | 17,500–40,000 words | Matches SFWA thresholds |
| Tor.com / Tordotcom Publishing | 20,000–40,000 words | Publisher-specific guideline (novella program) |

*We Heralds of Freedom* at ~30,161 words sits squarely in the novella range by every major
definition — comfortably above the floor and well below the ceiling.

### Adjacent Categories for Context

| Format | Typical Range |
|---|---|
| Novelette | 7,500–17,499 words (SFWA) |
| Novel | 40,000+ words (SFWA); commercial adult fantasy novels typically 90,000–120,000 words |

Debut adult fantasy novels are generally advised to stay under 120,000 words; epic/secondary-world
fantasy is the one subgenre where editors regularly accept 100,000–150,000 words from new authors.
The novella format sidesteps these length conventions entirely and is evaluated on different terms.

## 2. The U.S. Market for Fantasy Novellas

### Traditional Publishing

The novella occupies a narrow but increasingly respected niche in U.S. publishing. Key realities:

- **Tordotcom Publishing** is the dominant dedicated novella publisher in speculative fiction. Its
  novella program (20,000–40,000 words) has produced multiple Hugo, Nebula, and Locus Award winners.
  Tordotcom is an imprint of Tor Books (Macmillan). Submissions are primarily agent-only, though
  Tordotcom has occasionally opened brief submission windows for unagented authors. As of October
  2026 the novella program's guidelines page reportedly says it is closed to unagented, unsolicited
  novellas (see the agent research below).
- **Subterranean Press** publishes limited-edition novellas and shorter works, often from established
  authors, in handsome physical editions with collector appeal.
- **Neon Hemlock Press** is a small press publishing novellas in speculative fiction, often with
  literary and queer sensibilities; more accessible to debut authors.
- **Small and independent presses** (Lanternfish Press, Aqueduct Press, Fairwood Press) represent
  the most realistic path for debut novellas. Many accept unagented submissions during open reading
  periods.
- **Major imprints** (Tor, DAW, Ace, Orbit, Del Rey) very rarely acquire standalone novellas from
  debut authors. A novella manuscript submitted to a major house is almost always either (a)
  commissioned from an established author or (b) part of a multi-book deal.
- **Literary agents** are essential for Tordotcom and the major imprints. Agents with a speculative-
  fiction list (e.g., at Janklow & Nesbit, Howard Morhaim, Donald Maass, kt literary) are the
  standard path. Few U.S. agents say in writing that they take novellas, however. The October 2026
  research below lists those who do, those who exclude them, and their submission requirements.

### Literary Agents and Fantasy Novellas (Research Snapshot, October 2026)

This snapshot covers U.S. literary agents and where each stands on novellas: agents who say they take
them, agents whose fantasy wishlists fit this subgenre but who say nothing about novellas, and agents
who exclude them. Each entry gives the agent's reported fantasy interests and submission
requirements. "This subgenre" means the category in this file's title, with the political and
theological themes listed in §6. Researched October 25, 2026.

**Sourcing and verification.** Agency websites, wishlist pages, and query-tracking sites could not be
opened from the environment in which this section was compiled. Every item therefore comes from
web-search results that excerpt or summarize those pages:

- Words in quotation marks are as reported in those results and may differ slightly from the original.
- "(single report)" marks an item seen in only one search result. Unmarked items appeared in two or
  more results, sometimes drawn from the same page.
- "(phrase-checked)" means a search for the quoted words returned the named page; the surrounding
  sentence was not seen. "(phrase check failed)" means that search did not return the page. Such
  searches also miss wording that is present, so a failed check does not show the wording is wrong.
- Statements without quotation marks are paraphrases.
- Most agent pages carry no date. "Open" and "closed" are as reported, with a date where one was
  given. No status was confirmed live for October 2026.
- Duotrope, QueryTracker, QueryAcademy, LiteraryAgencies.com, and mswishlist.com are third-party
  listings and can lag behind an agent's own page. mswishlist.com collects #MSWL posts and is separate
  from the official manuscriptwishlist.com ("MSWL" below).
- Every agency below is a U.S. agency unless noted. Individual agents' home locations were not
  checked.

Check each agent's own page before querying.

**Main findings**

- **Written openness to novellas is rare.** Searches covered about 40 agencies, 24 of them checked
  agency by agency. Only one agency-wide length rule was found on an agency's own site, and it
  excludes novellas (JABberwocky); aggregator listings report a similar exclusion for Dunham
  Literary. The other agencies' submission pages give no rule either way, which means unknown, not
  yes.
- **Seven agents' profiles were reported to accept novellas or to carry a novella tag**, but most of
  these reports are unconfirmed or conflicting (table below). The clearest statements are Claire
  Lee's (The Kepner Agency; listed open as of about July 2026) and Jennifer Jackson's (Donald Maass;
  closed since July 2025).
- **No agent was found who is reported open, states in writing that novellas are welcome, and names
  political, court, or secondary-world fantasy** (inference from the entries below). Jennifer Jackson
  names both novellas and second-world fantasy but is closed.
- **Several agents with close subgenre matches say nothing about novellas.** Whether they would take
  one is untested; their live pages may settle it.
- **Agents do sell SFF novellas, mostly to Tordotcom**, but the deals found were largely sequels,
  follow-ons, or packages with novels (see the deal table). Duotrope's guide to searching for agents
  reportedly warns that very few agents represent novellas and suggests searching fiction publishers
  instead (single report; https://duotrope.com/guides/searching-agents/fiction).
- **Tordotcom's novella program reportedly requires an agent for now.** Its guidelines page
  (20,000–40,000 words) reportedly says it is closed to unagented, unsolicited novellas:
  https://tor.moksha.io/publication/tornovellas/guidelines

## 3. Manuscript Formatting (Submission Standard)

U.S. speculative-fiction publishers and agents expect **Standard Manuscript Format** (also called
Shunn format, after William Shunn's widely referenced guide):

| Element | Standard |
|---|---|
| Font | 12-point Times New Roman (or Courier New) |
| Spacing | Double-spaced throughout |
| Margins | 1 inch on all sides |
| Alignment | Left-aligned (ragged right), not justified |
| First-line indent | 0.5 inch (half-inch) for all body paragraphs |
| Scene breaks | Centered `#` on its own line |
| Header | Author surname / TITLE / page number (right-aligned, every page after page 1) |

| File format | .docx preferred by most agents/editors; some accept .rtf |

These conventions apply to agent queries and publisher submissions. Contest and award submissions may
specify their own formatting.

## 4. Craft Standards: Upmarket Character-Driven Fantasy

### What "Upmarket" Means

"Upmarket" in U.S. publishing denotes fiction that combines commercial readability (strong plot,
accessible prose, broad audience appeal) with literary sensibility (thematic depth, stylistic
ambition, psychological complexity). In fantasy, this translates to:

- **Prose quality** expected to be polished, rhythmic, and intentional — neither utilitarian genre
  prose nor self-consciously "literary" difficulty. Sentence-level craft is scrutinized.
- **Thematic depth** beyond the surface plot: the story means something beyond its events. Political,
  moral, spiritual, or philosophical questions are embedded in the narrative rather than appended
  to it.
- **Worldbuilding integrated with character**: the world exists to illuminate the characters'
  interiority, not as an end in itself. Exposition is earned through character need, not
  info-dumped.

### Character-Driven Structure in Novella Form

Character-driven fantasy novellas are distinguished from plot-driven ones by where the engine of the
story sits: in the protagonist's internal transformation, not in external events. The external
conflict (war, political crisis, magical threat) functions as a pressure that forces internal
change.

Structural expectations:

- **Single central conflict** with personal stakes tied to the protagonist's identity, beliefs, or
  relationships. Subplots exist but orbit the main arc.
- **Faster pacing than a novel** but **slower and more leisurely than a short story** — the novella's
  unique advantage is having room for character interiority, reflective passages, and relational
  development that a short story cannot afford, while maintaining a tighter narrative momentum than
  a novel.
- **Three-act structure** remains the default scaffold. In a ~30,000-word novella, a rough
  proportional guide:
  - Act I (setup/inciting incident): ~6,000–8,000 words
  - Act II (rising action/complications): ~15,000–18,000 words
  - Act III (climax/resolution): ~6,000–8,000 words
- **Limited POV cast**: one to three POV characters is standard for novellas. A single-POV novella is
  the most common and structurally cleanest form; dual-POV is also well established. More than three
  risks diffusing the intimacy that justifies the novella format.
- **Resonant ending**: character-driven novellas are expected to deliver emotional and thematic
  resolution even if external plot threads remain open. The reader must feel the protagonist has
  changed or arrived somewhere meaningful.

### What Editors and Agents Look For

In a character-driven fantasy novella submission, U.S. editors and agents evaluate:

1. **Voice** — a distinctive narrative voice is the single most important selling point for upmarket
   fiction at any length. The first page must establish it.
2. **Emotional core** — the reader must care about the protagonist's internal problem within the first
   few pages. Stakes should be personal before they are political or magical.
3. **Worldbuilding efficiency** — novellas cannot afford the expansive worldbuilding runway of a
   novel. The world must feel lived-in from the first scene, revealed through action and dialogue
   rather than exposition blocks.
4. **Prose control** — overwriting is the most common craft problem in fantasy novella submissions.
   Editors expect every sentence to earn its place. Purple prose, excessive adverbs, and
   redundant description are disqualifying at the query stage.
5. **Pacing** — a novella that reads like a compressed novel (too many subplots, too many characters,
   too much worldbuilding crammed in) or an inflated short story (thin premise stretched past its
   natural length) will not sell. The story must feel native to its length.

#### *Babel, or the Necessity of Violence: An Arcane History of the Oxford Translators' Revolution* — R.F. Kuang (2022)

**Facts**

- Publisher: Harper Voyager (US) and HarperVoyager (UK). Published August 23, 2022; about 544–560
  pages, depending on edition.
- Awards: Nebula Award for Best Novel (2022 award year, presented May 2023); Locus Award for Best
  Fantasy Novel (2023); British Book Awards, Fiction (2023).
- Sales: debuted at No. 1 on the New York Times hardcover fiction list.
- Hugo Awards: ruled "not eligible" for the 2023 Hugos without explanation, although nomination
  statistics released later showed enough nominations for the ballot (as reported by Locus and File
  770).

**Publisher's description (premise).** Retailer reproductions of the publisher copy open with
"Traduttore, traditore." In paraphrase, the copy runs as follows:

- In 1828, Robin Swift, orphaned by cholera in Canton, is brought to London by the mysterious
  Professor Lovell.
- He trains for Oxford's Royal Institute of Translation, "Babel," whose translation-based
  silver-working sustains the empire.
- The institute that first seems a paradise becomes a prison, and the copy asks whether a student can
  resist an empire.

The copy also calls the novel "a thematic response to The Secret History and a tonal retort to
Jonathan Strange & Mr. Norrell."

**What reviewers say, by element**

1. *Narrative structure and plot*
   - Chicago Review of Books (Daniel Rabuzzi, August 25, 2022): "brilliant both in concept and
     execution," "a page-turner with footnotes, a thriller with a higher purpose."
   - Reactor/Tor.com (Natalie Zutter, September 22, 2022): the novel makes its characters, and its
     readers, fall in love with an institution and then breaks their hearts; the ending is tragic but
     fitting (paraphrase).
   - Strange Horizons (Shinjini Dey, January 30, 2023): reports Kuang's own description of the book as
     "maximalist," citing its five-act structure, long descriptive paragraphs, and intricate magic
     systems (paraphrase of Dey's account).
   - *Consensus:* a coming-of-age campus story that turns into revolt against the institution, built
     on a large scale. Admirers find it propulsive; the reservations under narrative economy concern
     how often argument interrupts the story.
2. *Narrative economy*
   - Publishers Weekly (unsigned): the novel "underwhelms with a didactic, unsubtle take on dark
     academia and imperialism," and its narrative is "frequently interrupted by lectures on why
     imperialism is bad" (the second phrase via a secondary essay quoting the review).
   - Paste (Lacy Baugher Milas, August 23, 2022), headlined "An Ambitious Epic That Doesn't Trust Its
     Audience Enough": argues that the book does not let its horrors speak for themselves
     (paraphrase). Book Marks reproduces the line "a significant chunk of this doorstopper's 500+ pages
     feels didactic and lecture-y."
   - Kirkus Reviews: an "expansive, sympathetic, and nevertheless scathing critique of Western
     imperialism," with some criticism of how self-aware that critique is (via Book Marks; the
     reservation is a paraphrase).
   - Strange Horizons (Dey): ties the length to Kuang's "maximalist" design and its room "for
     indulgence and excess" (single report).
   - *Consensus:* the most consistent reservation across trade and magazine reviews is didacticism —
     exposition and argument interrupting the story across 500-plus pages. Admirers treat the scholarly
     apparatus (footnotes, epigraphs) as part of the design.
3. *Characterization*
   - Publishers Weekly: the characters "often feel dubiously motivated."
   - Slate (Laura Miller, August 2025, reviewing Kuang's *Katabasis* and looking back at *Babel*):
     "Kuang's abiding weakness as a novelist lies with character," with *Babel* cited as an example.
   - Enthusiastic reviews (Chicago Review of Books, Library Journal, BookPage) center the four student
     friends and the betrayal that divides them (paraphrase; single reports).
   - *Consensus:* divided. Trade reviews and later commentary find the characters subordinated to the
     thesis; enthusiastic reviews center the friendship.
4. *Worldbuilding*
   - Booklist (starred; via retailer reprints): a "richly descriptive stand-alone novel about an
     ever-expanding, alternate-world empire powered by magically enhanced silver talismans" that
     "scrutinizes linguistics, history, politics, and the social customs of Victorian-era Great
     Britain."
   - Kirkus Reviews: "Kuang draws a keen parallel between extracting knowledge and extracting
     resources, examining the terrible power of systems built on inequality" (via Book Marks).
   - The Guardian (Natasha Pulley, September 2022), headlined "an ingenious fantasy about empire":
     translation is the key to the magic, and Kuang uses the genre to sharpen a historical
     investigation into colonisation, learning, and power (paraphrase of the standfirst).
   - Paste: praises the book's "obvious depth of research" and its linguistics-based magic system (via
     Book Marks).
   - Chicago Review of Books: the invented world exposes the power dynamics of real history more
     sharply than many nonfiction histories (paraphrase).
   - *Consensus:* broad agreement that the translation-based silver magic is ingenious and bound to the
     empire's economy. The setting is historical (an alternate nineteenth-century Britain), not a
     secondary world.
5. *Theme*
   - The New York Times Book Review (Amal El-Mohtar, October 2022; listed as a rave by Book Marks): the
     book derives its power from "sustaining a contradiction" — loving and hating something that feeds
     on you (via secondary sources quoting the review).
   - Chicago Review of Books: the novel indicts, educates, and urges readers to reframe — to
     "(re)translate" — the dominant narrative of what the West calls its civilization (paraphrase with
     the reported word).
   - Library Journal: a "nuanced adventure" that blends historical speculative fiction with dark
     academia and critiques Western colonialism.
   - BookPage (starred): an "unforgiving examination of the cost of power."
   - Publishers Weekly (dissent): "Kuang assumes an audience that disagrees with her, and the result
     keeps readers who are already aware of the evils of racism and empire at arm's length."
   - *Consensus:* near-unanimous that empire, translation, complicity, and the costs of resistance are
     the core. The disagreement is over whether the argument is dramatized or asserted.
6. *Prose and style*
   - Library Journal (excerpt reproduced by Subterranean Press): "inventive and engaging, passionate
     and precise."
   - Paste: an incredible feat of writing that mixes etymology, history, and linguistics (paraphrase).
   - The Guardian (Pulley): "a scholarly book by a superb scholar" (single report).
   - Strange Horizons (Dey): the footnotes are few and mostly etymological, and read as a display of
     the book's claimed pedantry (paraphrase).
   - Publishers Weekly: "didactic, unsubtle" (see narrative economy).
   - *Consensus:* praise for the erudition and the mock-scholarly apparatus. Reservations target
     didacticism rather than sentence-level weakness.

choice but to take his place as the only surviving rightful heir." The copy adds that he has no
friends or advisors, and knows that whoever assassinated his father and brothers could strike at him
at any moment (paraphrase). One listing calls it a fantasy "of court intrigue and dark magics in a
steampunk-inflected world" (via an Apple Books listing).

**What reviewers say, by element**

1. *Narrative structure and plot*
   - Publishers Weekly (February 3, 2014): "less a novel than a series of anecdotes," yet "carried by
     the strength of atmosphere and Maia's resonant good-heartedness."
   - Kirkus Reviews (starred): "a plot full of small but deadly traps among which the sweet-natured,
     perplexed Maia must navigate. The result is a spellbinding and genuinely affecting drama" (via Book
     Marks).
   - io9 (Michael Ann Dobbs, May 23, 2014): "remarkably compelling and fascinating" despite consisting
     almost entirely of court intrigue. It "mostly eschews plot-heavy histrionics in favor of warmth,
     psychological depth, and hope" (via Wikipedia's account of the review).
   - Strange Horizons (Foz Meadows, May 26, 2014): the book opens with the crash of the airship
     *Wisdom of Choharo*. In her reading it moves toward the planned Wisdom Bridge — wisdom's death and
     rebirth (paraphrase; single report for the bridge framing). It ends on a muted sense of progress,
     trust, and healing rather than triumph (paraphrase via Wikipedia).
   - Fantasy Literature: "not a lot of action or romance: this is more of a tale of political intrigue
     and coming of age."
   - *Consensus:* a quiet accession-and-court-intrigue structure in which the assassination plot stays
     subordinate to Maia's adjustment to the throne. PW's "series of anecdotes" is the main structural
     reservation.
2. *Narrative economy*
   - Publishers Weekly and io9 (above) describe the pacing as episodic and low on histrionics.
   - Fantasy Literature: "The names were terribly confusing — at least until I discovered the glossary
     in the back of the book when I was halfway through."
   - Grimdark Magazine: the "onerous glossary" of characters, forms of address, and names is
     deliberate, so that readers feel as lost as the young emperor does (paraphrase with the reported
     phrase).
   - Reactor/Tor.com (CD Covington, May 5, 2020, on the book's names and vocabulary): the invented
     language is not extensively developed; the book uses a few dozen vocabulary words plus place names
     and titles of address (paraphrase; single report).
   - *Consensus:* critics treat the slow, low-action pacing as deliberate. The recurring cost is the
     density of invented names and titles, which the back matter (glossary and handbook) eases.
3. *Characterization*
   - Kirkus Reviews: "powerful character studies"; "the sweet-natured, perplexed Maia."
   - Strange Horizons (Meadows): "a poignantly sympathetic protagonist."
   - io9 (Dobbs): headlined "Goblin Emperor Has One Of The Most Lovable Characters We've Met In Ages."
   - Library Journal (starred; via the Tor/Forge blog): Maia "will tug on the heartstrings of even the
     most hardened reader."
   - Publishers Weekly: credits the portrayal of Maia's "shyness and insecurity" (single report for the
     fragment).
   - Tor.com (Liz Bourke, 2014): praised the "compelling attractiveness of Maia's character" as a
     fundamentally decent person (via Wikipedia).
   - Fantasy Literature: Maia's innate goodness is "balanced against his struggles to overcome his own
     impulses toward arrogance, vengeance, and fear" (single report).
   - *Consensus:* near-unanimous praise for Maia as decent, shy, and insecure. Secondary characters get
     little individual attention in professional reviews.
4. *Worldbuilding*
   - Publishers Weekly: "ambitious and meticulously executed" (via Book Marks).
   - Booklist (David Pitt; via retailer reprints): "Addison has built a completely believable world,
     with its own language, customs, and history, but there are tantalizingly familiar elements (such as
     newspapers and pocket watches)."
   - Strange Horizons (Meadows): "intricate worldbuilding," blending high fantasy with steampunk
     (paraphrase via Wikipedia).
   - Reactor/Tor.com (Covington): a consistent phonology and naming system, including title suffixes
     that mark rank (paraphrase; single report).
   - *Consensus:* the most consistently praised element — detailed, believable, steampunk-tinged. The
     naming system is admired but is also the main barrier to entry.
5. *Theme*
   - Strange Horizons (Meadows): "a novel about abuse and power" that handles "issues of race, class,
     gender, and sexuality in subtle but important ways" (via Wikipedia).
   - io9 (Dobbs): warmth, psychological depth, and hope (above); "a remarkably hopeful story of a single
     decent person doing his best in a difficult situation" (single report).
   - Lawyers, Guns & Money (Abigail Nussbaum, July 29, 2025): Maia's kindness is both a distinctive
     approach to high fantasy and a harbinger of the "cozy" trend, and the book "recognizes the limits
     of kindness" (paraphrase with the reported phrases). She also reads its politics as reform through
     institutions rather than their overthrow (single report).
   - Reactor/Tor.com essay "Hopepunk and Revolutionary Acts of Kindness" (2020): "one of the first books
     I read that felt truly hopepunk" (single report).
   - *Consensus:* critics converge on kindness and decency in power as the theme, often framed as
     hopeful or "hopepunk." The more analytical critics locate it in the relationship between abuse and
     power (Meadows) or in reform within institutions (Nussbaum).
6. *Prose and style*
   - Strange Horizons (Meadows): "elegant prose."
   - The Guardian (Francis Spufford): "witty, elegant, kind" (via Wikipedia; the original was not
     located).
   - Publishers Weekly: "carried by the strength of atmosphere."
   - Several reviews treat the formal register's pronoun system — a formal first-person "we" distinct
     from the plural, alongside informal "thou" forms — as part of the world's texture (paraphrase,
     drawn partly from non-professional reviews).
   - *Consensus:* professional descriptions of the prose are positive. No professional criticism of the
     prose appeared in the search results; complaints of stiffness appear in reader reviews, which are
     not used here.

#### Where the two comps converge and diverge

This summary is drawn only from the profiles above:

- **Common ground.** Both are novel-length political fantasies centered on one protagonist inside an
  institution: Oxford's translation institute in *Babel*, the imperial court in *The Goblin Emperor*.
  Reviewers praise both for worldbuilding bound to power, whether an empire run on translation-powered
  silver or a court of names, titles, and protocol.
- **Praise diverges.** For *Babel* it centers on ideas, research, and a magic system tied to empire.
  For *The Goblin Emperor* it centers on the protagonist's decency, the believability of the world, and
  the book's warmth.
- **Reservations diverge.** For *Babel* they are didacticism and characters subordinated to the
  thesis. For *The Goblin Emperor* they are an episodic, anecdotal structure and the density of
  invented names.
- **Endpoints diverge.** *Babel*'s subtitle names "the Necessity of Violence," and its copy frames
  revolt against an empire. Critics read *The Goblin Emperor* as hope and reform pursued through the
  institution itself.

## 6. Positioning Notes for *We Heralds of Freedom*

Based on the manuscript's characteristics and the standards above:

- **Word count** (30,161) is well-centered in the novella range. No adjustment needed.
- **Format**: the story's scope (one protagonist's arc across roughly two years, single nation, one
  central conflict with political/theological dimensions) is well-matched to novella length.
- **Character-driven credentials**: Carden's internal transformation (faith, marriage, vocation) is
  the engine; the geopolitical/theological conflict is the pressure — this is precisely the
  structure the market values.
- **Upmarket signals**: the manuscript's engagement with colonialism, religious plurality, economic
  justice, and institutional loyalty maps to the thematic ambition editors associate with "upmarket."
  The Arcane Tongue truth-forcing conceit is the kind of worldbuilding-as-metaphor that
  distinguishes upmarket from commercial fantasy.
- **Single POV** (Carden throughout; Marelyn is a non-POV supporting character experienced entirely
  through Carden's perspective) is the cleanest novella form.
- **Comp positioning**: the political-fantasy register and moral-conviction protagonist place this
  manuscript in conversation with *The Goblin Emperor*, *Babel*, and *Piranesi* — all of which
  foreground a single character's ethical stance against systemic power. The comp profiles in §5
  summarize what publishers and reviewers have said about *Babel* and *The Goblin Emperor* across six
  narrative elements.
- **Market path**: Tordotcom (via agent) is the aspirational target; small presses (Neon Hemlock,
  Lanternfish) and self-publishing are realistic alternatives for a debut. Award eligibility at
  this word count is a meaningful asset. The October 2026 agent research in §2 found few U.S. agents
  who state that they take novellas, and it lists them with their submission requirements.

# Assessment: Market Fit and Market Positioning

Evaluation-only assessment of *We Heralds of Freedom* for market fit and market positioning as a
complete narrative unit, as written in the `manuscript/` folder files (front-matter, chapters one
through eight, epilogue, and glossary), judged against the market standards, publishing landscape,
comparable titles, and positioning criteria compiled in `reference/industry-standards.md`.

This file is an assessment only and is not an authoritative source of narrative information. The
manuscript files in `manuscript/` are the sole authority; narrative claims below are anchored to them
by line reference (`chNN:line` for chapters, `ep:line` for the Epilogue, `front-matter:line` and
`glossary:line` for those files; 1-indexed file lines). Where a statement is an inference, an
interpretation, or an approximation, it is labeled as such.

Statements marked **(external)** describe market conditions, reader expectations, or the content of
comparable titles that `industry-standards.md` does not document. They cannot be verified against the
manuscript or the standard and are this evaluation's general observations; where the standard does
document a point, it is cited by section.

This assessment is diagnostic, not prescriptive. It identifies how the manuscript as written fits
within the current U.S. speculative-fiction market, where its positioning aligns with demonstrated
demand, and where its positioning presents challenges. It does not propose revisions or marketing
strategies.

Word counts are `wc -w` counts of the manuscript files as written (headings included): Ch1=3,232;
Ch2=4,224; Ch3=2,010; Ch4=3,871; Ch5=3,826; Ch6=3,025; Ch7=3,729; Ch8=2,997; Epilogue=3,247;
narrative body=30,161; total with front-matter (21) and glossary (665)=30,847.

## 2. Format and Word Count Fit

### 2a. Classification

The manuscript's narrative body of 30,161 words (30,847 with front-matter and glossary) sits within
the novella range by all three definitions cited in the standard:

| Definition | Range | Manuscript Position |
|---|---|---|
| SFWA / Nebula Awards | 17,500–39,999 words | 30,161 (within range) |
| Hugo Awards (WSFS) | 17,500–40,000 words | 30,161 (within range) |
| Tordotcom Publishing | 20,000–40,000 words | 30,161 (within range) |

The standard notes the manuscript is "comfortably above the floor and well below the ceiling" (§1)
and that its word count "is well-centered in the novella range" (§6). At 30,161 narrative words, the
manuscript sits just above the SFWA range's midpoint (28,750) and at about the midpoint of
Tordotcom's range (30,000). (The previous version placed it in the "middle-to-upper portion" of the
range; the arithmetic places it near the middle.) It is long enough to support the character
interiority and relational development the standard identifies as the novella's "unique advantage"
(§4), and short enough to remain clearly within the novella classification rather than approaching
the novel threshold of 40,000 words (§1).

### 2b. Format-to-Scope Match

The standard notes that "the story's scope (one protagonist's arc across roughly two years, single
nation, one central conflict with political/theological dimensions) is well-matched to novella
length" (§6). (The previous version quoted this line as "roughly one year"; the standard reads
"roughly two years.")

The manuscript as written is consistent with this:

- **Time span**: the narrative covers approximately two years (inference from time markers such as
  ch02:48, ch08:126, ep:32, and ep:102; see `assessment-manuscript.md` §8a). The previous version
  estimated eighteen to twenty-four months.
- **POV and setting**: it follows a single POV character within one nation, principally in two
  cities — Portsmouth, the capital, and Windbury.
- **Conflict and relationship**: it centers on a single conflict (Carden's moral conviction against
  institutional pressure) and one primary relationship arc (the Carden-Marelyn marriage)
  (interpretation).

In this evaluation's judgment, the manuscript does not exhibit the compression problems the standard
warns against — "too many subplots, too many characters, too much worldbuilding crammed in" (§4) —
nor the inflation problem of a "thin premise stretched past its natural length" (§4)
(interpretation; see `assessment-manuscript.md` §8c). The scope is native to the novella format.

## 3. Market Category Identification

### 3a. Primary Category

The manuscript fits within the market category **upmarket adult character-driven fantasy novella**.
Each element of this classification is anchored to manuscript content below.

**Upmarket**: The standard defines "upmarket" as fiction combining "commercial readability (strong
plot, accessible prose, broad audience appeal) with literary sensibility (thematic depth, stylistic
ambition, psychological complexity)" (§4). The manuscript demonstrates (interpretation):

- **Thematic depth**, through engagement with:
  - economic justice: the philanthropy-and-taxation arc across Ch2, Ch4, Ch6, Ch7, and Ch8;
  - religious plurality: three competing theologies with in-world governance implications — the
    government's pledge to the God of War (ch01:58), and the Creator's Scripture demanding that
    Alfainia "Dissolve your so-called governments" (ch05:95);
  - institutional critique: Carden's progression from institutional loyalist to conscientious
    objector;
  - a hierarchy among peoples: Elf slavery as systemic injustice (ch03:76, ch05:200, ep:10–18,
    ep:122–148).

  All of these are "embedded in the narrative rather than appended to it" (§4).
- **Prose quality**: sentence-level control across several registers (bureaucratic, domestic,
  emotional, liturgical) without the "Purple prose, excessive adverbs, and redundant description"
  the standard calls "disqualifying at the query stage" (§4).
- **Psychological complexity**: the marriage arc shows two people turning an arranged obligation into
  a genuine partnership, mostly through incremental, often non-verbal shifts. The Chapter Seven
  reveal (ch07:277–325) is the major exception.

**Adult**: The manuscript's concerns — marriage, career sacrifice, economic anxiety, parental
control, institutional loyalty, civic duty — are adult concerns (interpretation). The protagonist is
a middle-aged career professional. His age is inferred: he has been married twenty-five years
(ch01:210), worked at the Exchequer for "More than twenty years... Almost half his life" (ch07:205),
and spent "thirty-something years in the army and civil-service" (ep:48).

The narrative's content is moderate:

- violence is mostly recounted or remembered rather than dramatized (ch01:86, ch01:144, ch03:108–118,
  ch05:210), with one brief sword confrontation in the present (ch05:168–180);
- profanity is mild (e.g., "Damn it!" ch02:320; "grew the balls," ep:148);
- there is no explicit sexual content.

In this evaluation's judgment nothing pushes it beyond a general adult audience, while its thematic
preoccupations and emotional register are not addressed to young-adult or middle-grade readers
(interpretation).

**Character-driven**: The narrative's engine is Carden's internal transformation across three axes
(faith, marriage, vocation) rather than an external plot arc. The standard confirms: "Carden's
internal transformation (faith, marriage, vocation) is the engine; the geopolitical/theological
conflict is the pressure" (§6).

**Fantasy**: The manuscript has the following fantasy elements:

- a secondary world, named Eyael only in the glossary (glossary:37);
- a functional magic system (the Arcane and its many Magic types, glossary:5); the glossary also
  defines a second system, the Profane (glossary:61), which appears in the narrative only as idiom
  (e.g., ch03:34);
- non-human peoples (Dwarves, Elves);
- deities with active influence (the God of War, the Creator, the Father);
- supernatural phenomena (Scriptures, divine presence, self-actualization as magical progression).

These elements are integral to the narrative rather than decorative (interpretation).

**Novella**: Confirmed by the word count and scope analysis above.

### 3b. Subgenre Positioning

Within adult fantasy, the manuscript occupies the subgenre intersection that the standard identifies
in §6: **political fantasy** with a **moral-conviction protagonist** ("the political-fantasy register
and moral-conviction protagonist," §6).

The manuscript's political-fantasy credentials are grounded in specific content:

- Parliamentary governance: the investiture (ch01:4–74), budget references (ch06:24–26, ep:88), and
  taxation policy (ch01:158, ch05:110). No parliamentary debate is dramatized.
- Economic policy as narrative content: tax-exemptions, the war-bonds discussion (ch05:114–118), and
  the welfare-for-volunteering initiative (ch08:124–136, ep:16).
- Institutional power dynamics: Carden's relationship with Edward (Ch1–Ch7) and Horace's mayoral
  constraints (ch06:196, ch08:108–112, ch08:164, ep:88–94).
- Colonial and imperial structures: the Commonwealth-Dominion relationship (ch05:66–72), and Imperium
  Divinus as historical occupier and present threat (ch05:128, ch05:4, ch05:84–96).

The moral-conviction credentials rest on Carden's trajectory: a man who begins as an institutional
conformist ("A good soldier follows orders," ch01:88) and progressively chooses moral conviction at
personal cost (interpretation). He approves the Bakers' exemption (ch04:266) and the expanded
exemptions (ch06:128), accepts termination (ch07:183–207), and publicly advocates three-people
equality (ep:166–182). (The previous version omitted the Chapter Four approval.)

### 3c. Adjacencies and Overlaps

The manuscript shares characteristics with several adjacent market categories without belonging
squarely to any one.

**Faith-based fantasy**: Carden's faith in the Father is sincere, active, and central to his
decisions. The cathedral scenes include prayer (Ch6, ch06:152–166) and Scripture and prayer (Ch7,
ch07:124–141) that, on this reading, function as turning points (interpretation).

In this evaluation's judgment the manuscript does not fit the U.S. "Christian fiction" market
category (external), for these reasons:

- the theology is secondary-world: the Father is "one of the deities in Eyael's pantheon of gods"
  (glossary:39), and the Epilogue presents Him as uplifting the Creator, the God of War, and Lucifer
  (ep:120);
- the narrative does not evangelize (interpretation);
- the moral framework is embedded in character action rather than doctrinal statement
  (interpretation).

The Father's faith does carry recognizable echoes of Christian language: a "sole begotten Son" sent
"for the salvation of all Dwarves, Elves, and Humans" (ch02:270), a cross (ch06:164), "love is the
greatest commandment" (ch07:193), and a Scripture asking "What father when his son asks for bread
gives him a snake?" (ch07:126). Readers and editors may therefore read it as a Christian analogue
(interpretation). The manuscript's treatment of faith is closer to how literary fiction engages with
religion — as a dimension of character interiority — than to how the Christian-fiction market
packages it (external).

**Romance-adjacent**: The Carden-Marelyn marriage arc is the emotional core and could be described as
an "arranged marriage to love" trajectory. It departs from romance-genre conventions (external):

- there is no meet-cute or first kiss, since the marriage exists from the first page (ch01:12);
- the kisses shown are a forehead peck (ch05:40) and a kiss on the knuckles "for their friends to
  see" (ch08:30);
- no sexual intimacy is depicted.

The arc does end happily for the couple (ch07:323–335, ch08:210–212, ep:204–210). Its resolution is
demonstrated through shared domestic life and mutual sacrifice rather than through a romantic climax.
The manuscript is romance-adjacent in its emotional preoccupations but is not positioned for the
romance market (external).

**Military fantasy**: Carden's military background (Black Hounds service, ch01:120; mainland combat,
ch01:84–86, ch01:144) and the war-preparation context share elements with military fantasy. Combat in
the narrative present is limited to brief moments:

- Carden seizes a rock-thrower at the riot (ch02:292), and an explosion opens it (ch02:314);
- Julian draws a sword and Carden knocks it from his hands (ch05:168, ch05:180).

The three killings Carden reports are recounted afterward (ch03:108–118), and the Imperial flotilla
is only observed (ch03:138–162). The military background functions mainly as character history
rather than genre content (interpretation). (The previous version said "no combat scenes occur in the
narrative present.")

## 4. Comparable Title Analysis

### 4a. The Standard's Identified Comps

The standard's §6 places the manuscript "in conversation with *The Goblin Emperor*, *Babel*, and
*Piranesi* — all of which foreground a single character's ethical stance against systemic power."
The standard's §5 gives each a one-line description. This section compares each with the manuscript
as written. Characterizations of the comps beyond those one-line descriptions are marked (external).

**The Goblin Emperor (Katherine Addison, 2014)**: "political fantasy driven by a single character's
moral compass amid court intrigue" (§5). (The previous version paraphrased this as "institutional
intrigue.") The comp is strong on several axes:

- both protagonists navigate political systems they did not design;
- both make moral choices that defy institutional expectation;
- both narratives derive tension from bureaucratic and political pressure rather than combat or quest
  (interpretation; comp content external).

Carden uses courtesy strategically ("I better be my most charming with Horace," ch08:104; ch01:66),
which, on this reading, matches the comp's register (comp content external). The comp's limitation is
scope: *The Goblin Emperor* is novel-length (§5), with a larger cast and more intricate political
machinery (external). In the novella's compressed format, the political dimension operates at a lower
resolution (interpretation).

**Babel (R.F. Kuang, 2022)**: "upmarket historical fantasy, set in an alternate 1830s Oxford and
British Empire, engaging colonialism, language, and institutional power" (§5). The comp is strong on
the thematic axis. Both works foreground how
institutions extract value from their members, and how a language mediates power — here the Arcane
Tongue, used in government ritual (ch01:56), interrogation (ch03:66), and Edward's farewell
(ch07:199–201) (interpretation; *Babel*'s mechanism external). Both feature protagonists who begin as
institutional insiders and move toward dissent (Carden: ch01:92 to ch07:183–207).

The comp's limitation is tonal. In this evaluation's characterization, *Babel* operates in a register
of academic intensity and revolutionary anger (external), whereas this manuscript operates in a
register of sardonic bureaucratic observation and quiet faith (interpretation).

**Piranesi (Susanna Clarke, 2020)**: "literary fantasy, single POV, world-as-character,
introspective and puzzle-driven" (§5). The comp is relevant on structural grounds. Both works use a
single, deeply interior POV to build a world through subjective experience (interpretation), and both
trust the reader to construct understanding from accumulated observation. The comp's limitation is
genre register: *Piranesi* is puzzle-driven (§5) with little political content (external), whereas
this manuscript is explicitly political. The works share a structural philosophy more than a content
profile.

### 4b. Additional Comp Considerations Based on Manuscript Content

The manuscript also shares characteristics with titles cited in the standard's §5 that the §6
positioning notes do not invoke:

**Thornhedge (T. Kingfisher, 2023)**: Hugo Award for Best Novella, 2024 (§5). It is relevant as a
format comp: an award-winning fantasy novella. In this evaluation's characterization, *Thornhedge* is
an intimate, single-relationship-centered fairy-tale retelling with limited action (external). If so,
it shows that such novellas have current viability at the award level. The content differs (fairy
tale vs. political fantasy), but the format precedent applies.

**System Collapse / Murderbot series (Martha Wells)**: The standard lists *System Collapse* with the
Hugo Award for Best Novel (2024) and notes that the "Murderbot series novellas defined the modern
market" (§5). The series is the standard's example of a novella series in the market. The
manuscript's front-matter subtitle — "An Arcane Keys Novella" (front-matter:3) — signals series
branding. Whether the manuscript is intended as a standalone or a series entry is not stated in the
manuscript text; the subtitle implies series positioning (inference). §8e and §9f discuss the market
implications.

### 4c. Comp Positioning Summary

In this evaluation's judgment, the manuscript's strongest comp alignment is with **The Goblin
Emperor**. Both share political fantasy, a single moral-compass protagonist, an institutional
setting, tension from bureaucratic and ethical pressure rather than violence, and a hopeful arc
(interpretation; comp content external).

The previous version described this as an arc "in which the protagonist's moral conviction reshapes
the institution rather than destroying it." That overstates the manuscript. Carden is terminated
(ch07:183–207); the national institutions are unchanged — taxes remain high (ep:40, ep:72), and
Parliament has not reformed "the laws on Elf slavery" (ep:146). What Carden reshapes is local work
within a municipal institution: the City Hall welfare initiative (ch08:124–174, ep:4–28).

The Carden-Marelyn marriage arc adds a relational dimension that *The Goblin Emperor* does not
foreground as centrally (external), which provides differentiation.

The comps cluster along two axes (interpretation):

- political-fantasy institutional critique (*The Goblin Emperor*, *Babel*);
- intimate single-POV structure (*Piranesi*), with *Thornhedge* as the format precedent.

(The previous version called this a "two-comp formulation" while naming four titles, and framed it as
a pitch suggestion, which falls outside this assessment's non-prescriptive scope.)

## 5. Upmarket Credentials as Demonstrated in the Manuscript

The standard defines "upmarket" through three characteristics (§4). This section evaluates whether
the manuscript as written earns the "upmarket" designation on each.

### 5a. Prose Quality

The standard expects prose "polished, rhythmic, and intentional — neither utilitarian genre prose
nor self-consciously 'literary' difficulty" (§4). The manuscript demonstrates (interpretation):

- **Intentional variation**: the prose adjusts register by scene type:
  - bureaucratic precision in professional scenes ("With Laplace's included, that made it three firms
    this morning alone at risk of insolvency or drastic downsizing because of Edward's tax hike three
    months ago," ch02:48);
  - tactile specificity in domestic scenes ("Carden divested his anger, his bitterness, his despair
    into the poor carrots, celery, garlic, ginger, mutton, and onions," ch07:247);
  - restraint at emotional peaks ("Thank you." ch07:335).

  (The previous version's professional-scene example, ch05:222, is Marelyn's dialogue at the dinner
  table, not narration in a professional scene.)
- **Rhythmic control**: sentence length varies purposefully. In the investiture, the longer
  observational sentences of ch01:36–40 give way to the two-word "Oh no." (ch01:42). At the height of
  the Chapter Seven crisis, the prose drops to fragments: "Out." (ch07:215), "Clean." (ch07:217), "No
  job, no family, no support." (ch07:235), "Just him and the world now." (ch07:239).
- **Absence of common fantasy-prose faults**: in this evaluation's judgment, the manuscript does not
  exhibit the "Purple prose, excessive adverbs, and redundant description" the standard calls
  "disqualifying at the query stage" (§4).

The prose quality meets the "upmarket" threshold as defined by the standard.

### 5b. Thematic Depth

The standard expects "Thematic depth beyond the surface plot: the story means something beyond its
events," with "Political, moral, spiritual, or philosophical questions... embedded in the narrative
rather than appended to it" (§4).

The manuscript embeds several thematic concerns in its narrative action (interpretation):

- **Freedom as a multi-scalar concept**: "Heralds of Freedom" is part of a political formula in
  Chapter One (ch01:4–10) and of a declaration attributed to the Father in the Epilogue (ep:172).
  Between those points, freedom operates at several levels:
  - personal: liberation from parental control;
  - relational: choosing each other within an arranged marriage;
  - vocational: choosing one's work ("Free." ch07:207);
  - societal: Elf emancipation.

  These scales are demonstrated mainly through Carden's actions and choices. (The previous version
  described the relational level as "choosing a marriage partner"; the couple are married throughout
  and choose each other within the marriage.)
- **Institutional critique**: the manuscript traces how Alfainia's parliamentary government, civil
  service, and military become extractive under external pressure (the Imperial threat, rearmament
  taxation). The system is not presented as simply well-intentioned: Horace reveals that Parliament
  was "extremely generous with my campaign funds and my rivals conveniently ran into all sorts of
  legal issues" (ch03:52), and Carden thinks "Martial law went too far" (ch03:30). The
  critique is delivered through Carden's professional experience (the tax-exemption dilemma, the
  termination ultimatum) rather than abstract argument.
- **Economic justice**: the philanthropy subplot embeds a sustained argument about how wartime
  taxation strains civil society's capacity for mutual aid ("A noble effort now devoured by the
  ten-percent corporate tax hike," ch02:124). The argument is grounded in specific economic detail:
  tax revenues, exemption forms, stock prices, welfare queues. (The previous version said extraction
  "destroys" that capacity; the Bakers' program continues through the City Hall partnership,
  ch08:124–174.)
- **Religious plurality and sincere faith**: the three-deity framework and Carden's navigation of
  competing theological claims engage the relationship between faith, identity, and public life. The
  Arcane Tongue truth-forcing conceit gives this engagement a metaphysical grounding that a
  secondary-world fantasy can supply (interpretation; the previous version called it "unique to the
  fantasy genre," an external claim).

These thematic concerns meet the "upmarket" expectation of depth embedded in narrative rather than
appended.

### 5c. Worldbuilding Integrated with Character

The standard expects that "the world exists to illuminate the characters' interiority, not as an end
in itself. Exposition is earned through character need, not info-dumped" (§4).

The manuscript's worldbuilding is mostly introduced through Carden's professional and personal
encounters:

- the investiture through his attendance (ch01:4–74);
- Magic through the fraud case (ch02:30) and the Julian confrontation (ch05:142–198);
- the economic system through his tax work;
- the theological framework through successive Scripture encounters (ch01:132–140, ch05:84–96,
  ch07:124–129).

The manuscript contains no direct narratorial address to the reader. The previous version said no
section "pauses the narrative to explain the world." The closest exception is the Epilogue's Lucifer
passage (ep:108–128, ~240 words of retrospective summary), which `assessment-epilogue.md` §4c and
`assessment-manuscript.md` §6d assess as leaning toward the expository mode the standard cautions
against.

The standard's §6 identifies "The Arcane Tongue truth-forcing conceit" as "the kind of
worldbuilding-as-metaphor that distinguishes upmarket from commercial fantasy." The manuscript bears
this out. The Arcane Tongue functions both as a worldbuilding element — a language "only those born
with the Arcane's Magic could understand" (ch01:56; glossary:9) — and as a narrative device, binding
speakers to truth ("Arcane Tongue only allowed one to speak and write the truth," ch01:140) at
structurally critical moments. (The previous version described it as a language only Arcanists "can
speak"; the text specifies who can understand it. Whether the rule guarantees objective truth or
sincere belief is not settled by the text; see `evaluation-theme-and-worldbuilding.md` §12e.)

With the Epilogue exception noted above, the worldbuilding integration meets the "upmarket" standard
(interpretation).

### 5d. Summary of Upmarket Credentials

The manuscript earns the "upmarket" designation on all three of the standard's defining
characteristics: prose quality, thematic depth, and worldbuilding-character integration. This is a
market-positioning asset: "upmarket" is the descriptor the standard associates with combining
"commercial readability" and "literary sensibility" (§4).

## 6. Publishing Path Assessment

### 6a. Traditional Publishing — Tordotcom

The standard identifies Tordotcom Publishing as "the dominant dedicated novella publisher in
speculative fiction," with a novella program of 20,000–40,000 words that "has produced multiple Hugo,
Nebula, and Locus Award winners" (§2). The manuscript's 30,161 narrative words fall within
Tordotcom's range.

The standard notes that "Submissions are primarily agent-only, though Tordotcom has occasionally
opened brief submission windows for unagented authors" (§2). The Tordotcom path therefore requires
agent representation for most submission windows (inference from §2).

**Fit assessment based on manuscript content**: In this evaluation's judgment, the manuscript's
political-fantasy register, character-driven structure, thematic ambition, and prose control align
with a program the standard describes as award-producing. The standard's §6 names "Tordotcom (via
agent)" as "the aspirational target." That Tordotcom's catalog has featured novellas engaging
political systems, identity, and institutional power is an external observation.

**Positioning considerations**:

- The manuscript features a male protagonist in a secondary world whose institutions have British
  Empire parallels (interpretation). These parallels include an island-nation (glossary:17) with a
  Parliament and Prime-Minister (ch01:14–22), an Admiralty (ch05:34), and a Commonwealth with
  "overseas Dominions on all seven continents" (ch02:88).
- That Tordotcom's catalog trends toward diverse voices, and that the combination of male POV,
  sincere religious faith as a central trait, and marriage as the emotional core is less represented
  in its recent catalog, are external observations. The standard does not document Tordotcom's
  catalog composition.

Whether this distinctiveness is an asset (novelty) or a liability (unfamiliarity) is a
market-reception question that cannot be determined from the manuscript text.

### 6b. Traditional Publishing — Small and Independent Presses

The standard identifies small and independent presses as representing "the most realistic path for
debut novellas" (§2). It names Lanternfish Press, Aqueduct Press, and Fairwood Press as examples, and
says "Many accept unagented submissions during open reading periods" (§2). It describes Neon Hemlock
Press separately, as "more accessible to debut authors" (§2), and §6 names "small presses (Neon
Hemlock, Lanternfish)" as realistic alternatives. (The previous version said "These presses accept"
unagented submissions; the standard says "Many.")

**Fit assessment based on manuscript content**: The manuscript's thematic ambition, literary
sensibility, and character-driven structure align with the small-press path the standard describes
(interpretation). That small presses are often positioned to take risks on hard-to-categorize
manuscripts is an external observation.

**Positioning considerations**: The standard describes Neon Hemlock as "often with literary and queer
sensibilities" (§2). The manuscript does not foreground queer content; its central relationship is a
heterosexual marriage (ch01:12). This does not preclude Neon Hemlock, which §6 names as a realistic
alternative, but the manuscript's appeal there would rest on its literary qualities and thematic depth
(interpretation). Whether other small presses would be a more natural fit is an external judgment.

### 6c. Self-Publishing

The standard identifies self-publishing as "a viable and increasingly common path for novellas,
particularly in fantasy," with novellas "priced at $2.99–$4.99 (ebook) and $9.99–$14.99 (print)"
performing well (§2). It notes that "The 'novella series' model (multiple connected novellas released
in sequence) has strong commercial precedent in self-published fantasy" (§2).

**Fit assessment based on manuscript content**: The front-matter subtitle — "An Arcane Keys Novella"
(front-matter:3) — signals series branding. Whether the manuscript is intended as the first entry in
a novella series or as a standalone with series-universe framing is not stated in the manuscript
text. That the subtitle's form (series name plus "Novella") follows the usual commercial convention
for series entries is an external observation.

The manuscript's self-contained arcs coexist with open external threads:

- self-contained arcs: the marriage is resolved (ch07:323–335, ch08:210–212), the faith arc reaches
  its culmination (ep:166–192), and the vocational reconstruction is established (ch08:144–174,
  ep:4–28);
- open threads: the world war (ep:60–72), Elf emancipation (ep:126–128, ep:146), and Alfainia's
  political trajectory.

That this combination is the standard structure for series entries able to function both as
standalones and installments is an external observation.

**Positioning considerations**:

## 8. Market Strengths

The following elements of the manuscript as written are strengths for market positioning. Each is
anchored to specific manuscript content; comparisons with the wider market are marked (external).

### 8a. Distinctive Voice

The standard identifies voice as "the single most important selling point for upmarket fiction at any
length" (§4). Carden's narrative voice combines a sardonic institutional analyst, a reluctant
idealist, and a domestic caregiver who expresses emotion through cooking (interpretation). It is
established on the first page (ch01:10–12: "this most dreary and mundane of recurrent rituals"; "If
only his wife gave that much regard for their marital life") and sustained without significant breaks
across 30,161 words (interpretation).

The voice's distinctiveness rests on a combination of qualities in one narrative consciousness
(interpretation):

- political acuity (ch01:48, ch01:204);
- domestic tenderness (ch01:170–178);
- quiet faith (ch01:24, ch07:141);
- humor that is variously sardonic, wry, and self-deprecating (ch05:76; ep:84; ch02:88–90, ep:38).

That this combination is not well-represented in the current fantasy novella market is an external
observation.

### 8b. The Marriage Arc's Emotional Architecture

The manuscript tracks an arranged marriage's transformation into a chosen partnership across all nine
sections (Ch1–Epilogue), through incremental physical and verbal shifts. The arc's credibility rests
on accumulation rather than on dramatic romantic set-pieces (interpretation):

- warm fingers (ch02:248) and hot hands (ch04:84, ch07:331);
- shared meals and "Come eat" (ch02:192, ch04:22, ch04:280, ch06:74);
- shoulder massages (ch06:84, ep:40);
- conversations about their investments (ch01:184–198, ch05:222).

That this approach appeals to readers who seek emotional depth and relational realism in fantasy —
the readership behind works like *The Goblin Emperor* and *Piranesi* — is an external observation.

The Chapter Seven crisis sequence follows that accumulation. Carden misreads the cleaned-out flat
(ch07:213–297; about 43 paragraphs, ~1,070 words, approximate). The dinner revelation then shows that
Marelyn stayed with him and was disinherited by her father (ch07:283–291) rather than divorcing him
(ch07:323). (The previous version called the misreading "47-line" and said Marelyn "disinherited
herself"; the range holds 43 non-blank lines, and the form is "Signed by one Charles Winston,"
ch07:289.)

In this evaluation's reading, this is the arc's most compelling moment: a narrative surprise earned
by cumulative preparation, arising from the single POV rather than from a trick (interpretation; see
`assessment-manuscript.md` §10c).

### 8c. Worldbuilding-as-Metaphor

The standard identifies "The Arcane Tongue truth-forcing conceit" as "the kind of
worldbuilding-as-metaphor that distinguishes upmarket from commercial fantasy" (§6). The manuscript
deploys this conceit at its most consequential moments:

- Carden's declaration of faith to Julian (ch05:194–196); that it is in Arcane Tongue is inferred from
  ch05:170 and ch05:190;
- his justification to Edward (ch07:191–197);
- the Epilogue's three-people creedal expansion (ep:170–172).

The self-actualization mechanic is a second layer of worldbuilding-as-metaphor. In it, an Arcanist
progresses by living a Calling's ideals; the glossary describes "a lived, experiential process where
an Arcanist or Profaner must embody and live the ideals of their Calling to progress it"
(glossary:73). Contrasting this with progression through combat prowess or study is an
interpretation; the glossary also mentions a "Calling-specific feat" required "at certain Tiers"
(glossary:23).

In this evaluation's reading, these conceits make the magic system a thematic argument about
sincerity, personal growth, and collective action, not merely a power framework (interpretation).
That this appeals to the readership behind *Babel*, *Piranesi*, and *Jonathan Strange & Mr Norrell*
is an external observation.

### 8d. Low-Combat Political Fantasy

The manuscript is a political fantasy novella in which the central conflicts are resolved through
moral choice, bureaucratic action, and community-building rather than combat (interpretation).
Carden's most consequential acts are:

- approving the Bakers' exemption (ch04:266);
- signing the expanded exemption forms (ch06:128);
- signing his termination letter (ch07:183);
- delivering a speech to a mixed congregation of Elves, half-Elves, and Humans (ep:106, ep:166–182).

The Julian confrontation (ch05:142–198) is the most extended confrontation dramatized in the present.
It ends without a fight: Zeal Magic erodes Julian's confidence, Carden knocks the trembling sword from
his hands (ch05:180), and Julian leaves. The cut Carden later finds (ch05:198) shows it was not
bloodless. Elsewhere, Carden seizes a rock-thrower at the riot (ch02:292) and recounts killing three
people that day (ch03:108–118).

The previous version said the Julian confrontation is resolved "through verbal challenge and Zeal
Magic's truth-forcing properties rather than through physical violence." Truth-forcing belongs to the
Arcane Tongue (ch01:140), not Zeal Magic, and the confrontation includes a drawn sword and a wound. It
also called the approach "non-violent"; "low-combat" fits the text better.

This low-combat approach to political fantasy is a market differentiator (interpretation). That *The
Goblin Emperor* demonstrated the readership appeal of political fantasy centered on institutional
navigation, courtesy, and moral conviction is an external observation. The manuscript occupies
similar ground, with the added dimensions of the marriage arc and the faith theme.

### 8e. Series Potential

The front-matter subtitle "An Arcane Keys Novella" (front-matter:3) signals series branding. The
manuscript's self-contained character arcs, combined with open external threads, create natural entry
points for subsequent novellas (interpretation). The open threads include:

- the world war (ep:60–72);
- Elf emancipation (ep:126–128, ep:146);
- Alfainia's political trajectory (ep:88–94);
- the Lucifer revelation's implications (ep:110–128).

The glossary's scope supports this reading. Of its 40 entries, about 16 name terms that do not appear
in the narrative chapters — for example "Worldheart," "Spawning," "Profane Floods," "Beastman," and
"Pértus" (approximate, by term search). The glossary itself refers to "the admittedly many unique
terms in the book" (glossary:3). That this indicates a fictional universe developed beyond this
novella's needs is an inference.

The standard notes that "The 'novella series' model... has strong commercial precedent in
self-published fantasy" (§2), and that a novella submitted to a major house is almost always
"commissioned from an established author or... part of a multi-book deal" (§2). Series potential is
therefore relevant on more than one publishing path (interpretation).

## 9. Market Risks and Positioning Challenges

The following elements of the manuscript as written are challenges for market positioning. Each is
anchored to manuscript content; claims about market trends are marked (external).

### 9a. Male Protagonist in the Current Market

That the current U.S. fantasy market, particularly at the upmarket and award-competitive level, has
trended toward female, nonbinary, and marginalized-identity protagonists is an external observation.
So is the note that male-POV works continue to be published, nominated, and awarded. The standard does
not document protagonist demographics. The manuscript features a male protagonist in a heterosexual
marriage (ch01:12). If the external trend holds, the protagonist's identity does not provide the kind
of identity-based differentiation associated with recent upmarket success (external).

This is a market-positioning observation, not a craft assessment. The manuscript's characterization of
Carden is itself a distinctive portrait of masculinity in fantasy (interpretation):

- a man who cooks as an expression of care (ch01:170–178, ch07:245–247);
- a man who often processes emotion through physical action rather than verbal declaration
  (ch07:333–335);
- a man who grows into domestic partnership and community service (ep:4–58).

Whether agents and editors frame this as a differentiator or a category mismatch is a reception
question.

### 9b. Sincere Faith as Central Character Trait

Carden's faith in the Father is central to his identity and decision-making:

- he prays at turning points (ch06:166, ch07:16, ch07:141);
- he invokes the Father's teaching as justification for professional sacrifice (ch07:193);
- he thanks the Father for Marelyn's presence in his life (ch07:329, ch08:118).

The Epilogue's climactic speech is framed in the Father's teachings and command (ep:166, ep:172,
ep:178–180) and delivered in Windbury Cathedral (ep:102). (The previous version cited ch07:193–194;
the declaration is at ch07:193.)

That the mainstream U.S. SFF market generally treats religion in fantasy as a power system, a subject
of critique, or cultural texture is an external observation; the standard does not describe these
lenses. In this evaluation's reading, Carden's faith is personal, sincere, and positively
transformative. It functions more like the way literary fiction treats religion — as a dimension of
character interiority that shapes moral reasoning — than like those three lenses (interpretation).

This positioning is distinctive but carries risk. Some agents and editors may find sincere religious
faith in a protagonist unfamiliar or difficult to categorize; others may see it as an
underrepresented dimension and thus a differentiator (external). The manuscript does not evangelize
(interpretation). It presents faith as one character's lived experience within a secondary-world
theology whose language echoes Christianity (§3c), and the sincerity and centrality of that faith is a
market-positioning variable.

### 9c. Low-Action Narrative

The manuscript's conflicts are resolved mainly through moral choice, bureaucratic decision, and
community-building rather than combat, magical confrontation, or physical danger (interpretation).
The brief confrontations in the narrative present are:

- the Julian confrontation (ch05:142–198), with a drawn sword (ch05:168) and a disarm (ch05:180);
- the opening moments of the riot (ch02:288–328).

The three killings are recounted afterward rather than dramatized (ch03:108–118). The air-raid is
narrated in real time (ch03:128–162) but involves no engagement: the Imperial flotilla withdraws behind
a conjured storm (ch03:158–162). (The previous version said the air-raid is "narrated in compressed
retrospective"; it is narrated as it happens.)

Fantasy readers who expect action set-pieces, magical combat, or physical danger as genre staples will
not find them here (external). The manuscript's tension is domestic, professional, and moral
(interpretation). This is consistent with the upmarket character-driven category, but it may narrow
the potential readership within the broader fantasy market (external). That *The Goblin Emperor* and
*Piranesi* are similarly low-action is also external.

### 9d. Romance-Adjacent Without Romance Payoffs

The marriage arc is the manuscript's emotional core, but it does not deliver conventional romance
beats (external, as to genre conventions):

- there is no romantic first kiss; the kisses shown are a forehead peck (ch05:40) and a kiss on the
  knuckles (ch08:30);
- there is no exchange of "I love you"; Carden's "my love" (ch08:30) is an endearment;
- there is no sexual intimacy scene.

In this evaluation's reading, the arc's most romantic moments are understated: the hill scene
(ch08:70–98), the jointly spoken "We'll make this work" (ch08:210–212), and the conjured purse
(ep:202–210) (interpretation). Marelyn's "I was never going to divorce you" (ch07:323) is the arc's
most declarative moment, but it is a statement of commitment rather than a declaration of love.

Readers drawn by the arranged-marriage premise may expect romance-genre payoffs the manuscript does not
provide (external). The previous version said the manuscript "deliberately withholds" them; authorial
intent is an inference. This restraint is consistent with the upmarket positioning
(interpretation). It may, however, place the manuscript between two audiences (external): romance
readers who may find it emotionally unsatisfying, and literary-fiction readers who may not expect a
marriage arc at the center of a political fantasy.

### 9e. Worldbuilding Density

The glossary contains 40 entries covering world systems, geography, creatures, magic terminology, and
cosmology; about 16 do not appear in the narrative (§8e). The narrative itself introduces:

- the investiture ritual;
- three deities;
- multiple Magic types;
- a multi-continental geopolitical framework;
- three peoples;
- a theological history spanning millennia ("imprisoned for millennia," ep:120; "millennia of
  penance," ep:122);
- the Lucifer revelation.

In this evaluation's judgment, this worldbuilding is mostly delivered efficiently, through character
need rather than exposition blocks (with the exception noted in §5c). Even so, the density of the
secondary world is substantial for a ~30,000-word novella (interpretation).

The standard warns that "novellas cannot afford the expansive worldbuilding runway of a novel" (§4).
The manuscript manages its worldbuilding load through selective revelation and cumulative
construction. Still, a reader of Chapter One meets a lot of world-information: the investiture, the
God of War's divine presence, the Dwarf-Human compact, the magic system (Arcane Tongue, Demigods), the
political structure, and the marital dynamic. (The previous version also listed "the Elf absence";
Chapter One does not mention Elves, so the absence is not information a reader meets there.)

The glossary acknowledges the load directly: it is "Entirely skippable, but posted here if readers
would appreciate refreshers for the admittedly many unique terms in the book" (glossary:3). (The
previous version inferred this awareness from the glossary's inclusion; the glossary states it.)

This density is not inherently a market liability, since complex secondary worlds are expected in
fantasy (external). It does position the manuscript toward readers comfortable with worldbuilding
investment rather than those seeking immediate accessibility, which may limit its appeal to casual or
crossover readers (external).

### 9f. The "Arcane Keys" Series Question

The front-matter subtitle "An Arcane Keys Novella" (front-matter:3) raises a market-positioning
question the manuscript text does not answer: is this a standalone or the first entry in a series?

In the **traditional-publishing path**, the standard notes that major imprints "very rarely acquire
standalone novellas from debut authors," and that a novella submitted to a major house is almost
always either "commissioned from an established author or... part of a multi-book deal" (§2). That
standalone and series-entry novellas are otherwise pitched differently — a series entry requiring the
author to articulate the series arc and subsequent entries — is an external observation.

The manuscript as written can function as either (interpretation):

- standalone-capable: its character arcs resolve (ch07:323–335, ch08:210–212, ep:184–210);
- series-capable: its open world-state threads provide sequel hooks (§8e).

The submission framing differs depending on which the author chooses.

In the **self-publishing path**, the standard notes that the "novella series" model has "strong
commercial precedent" (§2). That a self-published series entry benefits from read-through across a
multi-entry catalog, while a standalone competes on individual-title appeal, is an external
observation.

The manuscript text does not resolve this question. The assessment notes it as a positioning decision
that sits outside the manuscript's content.

## 10. The Front-Matter Tagline as Market Signal

The front-matter's tagline — "A story of Mankind, the Father's Heralds of Freedom" (front-matter:6) —
signals the manuscript's thematic center in compact form (interpretation). The tagline foregrounds
two dimensions:

- theological identity: the Father's framework, not the God of War's or the Creator's;
- collective identity: "Mankind" as a people defined by a creedal title, matching the creed "Mankind,

As a market signal, the tagline points to the manuscript's faith-engaged, identity-centered thematic

or the institutional critique. Because it names only Mankind, it also does not signal the Epilogue's
extension of the creeds to Dwarfkind and Elfkind (ep:172).

Whether the tagline serves the manuscript's market positioning depends on which elements an agent,
editor, or reader considers primary. In this evaluation's reading, it foregrounds what is most
distinctive about the manuscript (the sincere faith engagement) rather than what is most immediately
accessible to the upmarket fantasy market (the political-institutional dimension and the relationship
arc) (interpretation).

This is an observation about the tagline's market function, not an assessment of its literary
quality.

## 12. Summary Assessment

### 12a. Market Fit

*We Heralds of Freedom*, as written in the manuscript files, fits within a viable market category: the
upmarket adult character-driven fantasy novella. Its word count, format, scope, and craft meet the
category's defining standards (§1, §4, §6; craft as assessed in `assessment-manuscript.md`). The
standard describes the novella as occupying "a narrow but increasingly respected niche in U.S.
publishing" (§2), with established publishing paths (Tordotcom, small presses, self-publishing) and an
awards pipeline (§2).

### 12b. Market Positioning

The manuscript's market positioning rests on a distinctive combination of elements (interpretation):

- low-combat political fantasy (cf. *The Goblin Emperor*);
- marriage as the emotional core in a non-romance framework;
- sincere religious faith as a positive character trait (external, as to how secular SFF receives it);
- worldbuilding-as-metaphor (the Arcane Tongue, self-actualization);
- a bureaucratic-professional setting (tax policy, civil service, municipal governance);
- a sardonic yet tender narrative voice;
- institutional critique that ends in local reconstruction rather than destruction.

This combination differentiates the manuscript from its closest comps and places it in a specific
niche. That the niche is underserved — few current novellas combine all these elements — is an
external observation. If accurate, the manuscript has limited direct competition but also limited
proven commercial precedent for its exact profile.

### 12c. Primary Market Strengths

1. **Voice as selling point**: the first of the standard's editorial criteria (§4), met from the
   first page.
2. **Emotional architecture**: the marriage arc's incremental credibility (interpretation).
3. **Thematic depth**: several embedded themes; their alignment with current market interest in
   institutional critique, economic justice, and equity is external.
4. **Format fit**: word count and scope native to the novella, with series potential signaled by the
   subtitle (front-matter:3).
5. **Comp positioning**: thematic alignment with *The Goblin Emperor* and *Babel*, and format
   alignment with *Thornhedge* (interpretation; comp content external).

### 12d. Primary Market Risks

1. Male protagonist in a market segment trending toward other protagonist identities (external).
2. Sincere faith engagement as an unfamiliar element in secular SFF (external).
3. A low-action narrative that may limit the broader fantasy readership (external).
4. A romance-adjacent emotional core without romance-genre payoffs.
5. Worldbuilding density that is substantial for novella length (interpretation).
6. The unresolved series-versus-standalone positioning question.

### 12e. Publishing Path Alignment

| Path | Fit | Key Consideration |
|---|---|---|
| Tordotcom (via agent) | "the aspirational target" (§6); content and quality align (interpretation) | Requires agent for most windows (§2); catalog positioning (external) |

### 12f. Concluding Observation

The manuscript as written offers a market profile of high distinctiveness and moderate positioning
complexity (interpretation). Its craft quality, thematic depth, and emotional architecture meet the
upmarket standard and position it competitively within the fantasy novella field. Its distinctive
elements — the faith engagement, the marriage arc, the low-combat political register, the
bureaucratic setting — differentiate it from the existing field. They also require agents and editors
to recognize these elements as assets rather than category complications (external). The manuscript's
market success will depend in significant part on finding industry advocates who read its
distinctiveness as freshness rather than difficulty of categorization (external).

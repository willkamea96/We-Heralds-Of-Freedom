# Character Assessment: Marelyn Winston (Oakmoore)

Evaluation-only assessment of Marelyn's manuscript-level characterization as a non-POV supporting
character, judged against the craft standards compiled in `reference/industry-standards.md`. (The
label "non-POV supporting character" follows `industry-standards.md` §6; her non-POV status is a
fact of the manuscript, while "supporting" rather than co-lead is a categorization this assessment
adopts — see §3b.)

This file is an assessment only and is not an authoritative source of narrative information. The
manuscript files in `manuscript/` are the sole authority; narrative claims below are anchored to them
by line reference (`chNN:line` or `ep:line` for the Epilogue, 1-indexed). Counts of recurring words
or gestures are counts of the manuscript files at the time of this assessment. Where a statement is
an inference, an interpretation, or an approximation, it is labeled as such.

This assessment is diagnostic, not prescriptive. It identifies what the Draft Zero characterization
achieves, where it meets or exceeds the upmarket adult character-driven fantasy standard, and where
gaps or risks exist at the craft level. It does not propose rewrites.

---

## 1. POV Status: Confirmed Non-POV

Marelyn never serves as a point-of-view character. Every scene in the novella is filtered through
Carden's close third-person perspective. The reader experiences Marelyn exclusively through:

- Carden's direct observation of her physical behavior (trembling, clenching, eye movements, facial
  expressions, body temperature — e.g., ch04:84–88, ch04:98, ch04:196, ch07:331)
- Her dialogue
- Carden's interpretive narration of her emotional state
- The results of her off-page actions, reported after the fact (e.g., ch05:218, ch07:277–291)
- Other characters' commentary about her (Horace, ch02:80; Jane, ch04:76–78; Beatrice, ch04:148,
  ch04:190; Charles, ch04:186)

This is the correct structural choice for a character-driven novella at this word count (30,161
narrative words). The industry standard for novellas is "one to three POV characters," with "A
single-POV novella... the most common and structurally cleanest form" (`industry-standards.md` §4).
Marelyn's non-POV status preserves the narrative intimacy the novella format demands while allowing
her to function as a secondary engine of the story (interpretation).

## 2. Characterization Technique: Evaluation Against Craft Standards

### 2a. Indirect Interiority (Strength)

The manuscript's primary characterization technique for Marelyn is **indirect interiority** — the
reader infers her inner life from physical signals observed through Carden's limited, often
misreading perspective. This technique is consistently applied and represents one of the
manuscript's strongest craft achievements.

Examples of the technique at its most effective:

- **Ch1**: "Marelyn scoffed and leaned on Carden, their long-agreed-upon modicum of intimacy for the
  public eye. She felt stiff as a board and cold as a stone" (ch01:68). The performative intimacy and
  the physical cold communicate a 25-year emotional distance without exposition (the 25-year figure
  is from ch01:210).
- **Ch2**: Marelyn's reaction when Carden tells her that Annie, his "old flame," is "happily married
  to one Roger Baker" (ch02:216–220) is rendered entirely through somatic cues. Marelyn already knows
  Roger: she gives an impeccably accurate physical description of him (ch02:222–224), and only then
  breaks down. She pats the spot next to her on the couch, locks her arms around Carden's arm "as if
  clinging to a mast," and his skin goes "wet under the sleeve" (ch02:226). Her three questions ("Why
  do we both need to live like this?" / "Why won't things ever go my way?" / "Why can't I be me?";
  ch02:232–236) arrive without attribution to a specific trigger, so the reader feels their
  accumulated weight. Carden's own recognition — "How preposterous he only learned after
  twenty-five years Marelyn did too" (ch02:242) — completes the scene. That the trigger is Roger's
  marriage rather than Annie's existence is this evaluation's inference from the sequence, supported
  by later text: Roger's startled "Marelyn… Winston?" and his memory that she "loved the
  hazelnut-cream croissants" (ch04:250–262), her worry that Roger "intended some type of advance on
  me" (ch06:92), and the Ch8 hilltop confession that, in the years Carden and Annie were teenagers
  (ch08:78), she "nagged a certain young baker at his family's downtown bakery for free croissants
  every morning" and was "instantly
  smitten by someone who indulged me without asking for anything in return" (ch08:84–86). The
  manuscript does not explicitly confirm the link, and her questions ("Why can't I be me?")
  encompass broader frustrations that the Roger revelation contextualizes rather than fully explains.
- **Ch4, Oakmoore estate**: "Marelyn's fingers surreptitiously laced between Carden's and clenched
  his hand so hard her knuckles turned white, yet her face remained implacable if outwardly genial
  as always" (ch04:98). The split between public composure and private desperation is communicated
  in a single image.
- **Ch7**: The overnight silence from Marelyn's locked study — "the only things that emerged from
  Marelyn's study were sobs, sniffles, and wails" (ch07:173) — communicates a crisis the reader is
  never allowed inside. This is the manuscript's most effective use of the non-POV constraint as a
  source of narrative tension rather than a limitation (interpretation).

**Assessment**: This indirect-interiority technique meets the upmarket standard. It demands active
reading — the audience must interpret physical evidence rather than being told what Marelyn feels.
The industry standard defines "upmarket" as combining "commercial readability" with "literary
sensibility (thematic depth, stylistic ambition, psychological complexity)" (`industry-standards.md`
§4); the evaluation reads this technique as an instance of that psychological complexity
(interpretation).

### 2b. Dramatic Irony via Carden's Misreading (Strength)

The manuscript's most sophisticated characterization move is using Carden's unreliable
interpretation of Marelyn as a source of dramatic irony. The reader can see what Carden cannot —
or, more precisely, what Carden sees but misinterprets.

The Ch7 emptied-flat and dinner sequence (ch07:213–299) is the clearest example. Carden interprets
every signal incorrectly:

- Marelyn's belongings are gone → she's left him (ch07:217–237)
- A second set of papers on the table → "was Julian that petty he needed to legally disinherit
  Carden twice over?" (ch07:263)
- Marelyn is smiling, glowing, moving "for some odd reason... with a certain languid grace" → she
  "must have won a tremendous amount of alimony from him at court" (ch07:265)
- Her wedding ring is still on → "At least she was still considerate enough to keep up the pretense
  one last time" (ch07:267)

Every inference is wrong. Marelyn packed "*our* things" and "got a lovely deal for my purses"
(ch07:277–279); the second form is a disinheritance from the Winston family "Signed by one Charles
Winston" (ch07:289), which, she says, "we filed... at court" — "the look on father's face was
priceless" (ch07:283). Who "we" refers to is not specified. Her father's condition — "if you insist
on staying with a civil-servant terminated in disgrace and disowned by his own family, then feel
free to join him in those circumstances" (ch07:291) — shows the disinheritance was the price of
staying with Carden. She is glowing because she chose him (interpretation, supported by
ch07:307–317). The reader who has been tracking Marelyn's physical signals across earlier chapters —
the clenched hands, the performative composure, the quiet tears, the growing warmth — can sense the
mismatch before the reveal lands (interpretation).

**Assessment**: This is the kind of scene-level craft that distinguishes upmarket from commercial
fantasy (interpretation; `industry-standards.md` §4 does not single out dramatic irony, but defines
upmarket by "psychological complexity" among other qualities). It uses the non-POV constraint
generatively: Marelyn's interiority is more powerful for being hidden behind Carden's misreading. The
reveal ("Why in the Father's name would I divorce you?", ch07:297) earns its emotional impact
because the reader has been accumulating evidence the POV character has not.

### 2c. Dialogue Voice (Strength with a Qualification)

Marelyn's dialogue is distinctive and consistently characterized. Her speech patterns include:

- **Weaponized inaneness**: "Can't believe my family skipped this for afternoon tea" (ch01:68) —
  delivered "in the blandest of tones" to complete the cover story Carden started ("Something about
  afternoon tea?", ch01:66) after her muttered "Traitors" (ch01:62); "At least the lie worked"
  (ch01:70). Carden names the inane register explicitly in Ch2 (below).
- **Multilayered questions**: "What do you think about all this?" (ch01:198) and "What do you think
  about all this, Carden?" (ch05:226) — Carden observes that "How Marelyn managed to utter such
  multilayered questions with such inaneness, Carden could never understand. He only knew in these
  moments Marelyn demanded the truth even as she herself skirted the real question" (ch02:218).
- **Blunt practicality**: "Then just do the right thing. Sign the exemption forms, drop them off at
  Edward's desk, see what happens" (ch06:116) — her directness cuts through Carden's equivocation;
  "Why must you always make things so complicated?" (ch06:124).
- **Emotional precision under duress**: "From the moment our fathers told me what happened yesterday,
  my heart already knew what we should do" (ch07:307) … "Except it wasn't ready yet for the
  consequences" (ch07:309).

The qualification: Marelyn's dialogue is often mediated by Carden's interpretive framing. The reader
frequently encounters her words with Carden glossing them ("How Marelyn managed to utter such
multilayered questions," ch02:218; "for some odd reason Marelyn flinched," ch04:42; "Marelyn said in
the flattest of tones," ch04:156). This is structurally correct for Carden's close-third POV, but it does risk creating
the impression that Marelyn's speech requires Carden's explanation to be legible. In some instances,
the dialogue is strong enough to stand on its own, and the interpretive frame slightly undermines
the reader's trust in their own ability to read Marelyn — which is the trust the dramatic irony
in Ch7 depends on.

**Assessment**: Meets the standard. Marelyn's voice is distinct from Carden's, from Annie's, and
from the other female characters (Jane, Beatrice) (interpretation). She speaks like someone who has
spent 25 years learning to communicate around obstacles (interpretation). The qualification is a
craft refinement, not a deficiency.

### 2d. Off-Page Agency (Strength)

One of the manuscript's most effective characterization decisions is having Marelyn act off-page
during pivotal moments and then presenting the results to Carden (and the reader) after the fact.
This technique preserves the single-POV structure while giving Marelyn autonomous agency.

Major off-page actions:

- **Ch3–4**: After the riot, Marelyn sends her own priority telegram — "I am coming on the first
  available Sunday train. Please wait for me" (ch03:170) — and arrives in Windbury unbidden (ch04:4–6).
- **Ch5**: On her own proposal (ch05:34–36), while Carden deals with Julian's attack and the
  Creator's Scripture crisis, Marelyn gathers intelligence from her father Charles in Parliament,
  her brother Jeremy in the Admiralty, the officers' wives in the Admiralty's social club, and her
  married female friends from the old nobility (ch05:218). She returns with a geopolitical picture —
  "Everyone's gone mad" (ch05:208), "Everyone wants war" (ch05:214), and the Stock Exchange's
  evidence that "Every sector is hemorrhaging except war-related industries" (ch05:222) — that Carden
  could not have assembled alone (interpretation).
- **Ch6**: Marelyn verifies the legitimacy of the Bakers' partnership through her own knowledge of
  the "widely accredited" third-party auditor (ch06:112) — information Carden lacks — after Roger
  approached her at the Stock Exchange (ch06:88–92).
- **Ch7**: On the day Carden is terminated (ch07:177–211), Marelyn — unknown to Carden and the reader
  — packs their things, sells her purse collection, and files the Winston disinheritance at court
  (ch07:277–291). The reader experiences this as a Ch7 reveal, not as a narrated sequence.
- **Ch8**: She has already "sorted everything out" in their finances (ch08:28).

**Assessment**: This is structurally exemplary for a non-POV supporting character. It solves the
classic problem of the "waiting partner" — the supporting character who exists only in relation to
the protagonist's scenes (the "waiting partner" framing is this evaluation's, not
`industry-standards.md`'s). Marelyn's off-page actions demonstrate autonomous decision-making and
independent competence. The delayed reveal of her Ch7 actions is, in this evaluation's judgment, the
manuscript's single most powerful character moment.

### 3b. Arc Assessment

This is a complete, internally coherent character arc that tracks from emotional imprisonment to
self-liberation (interpretation). Marelyn's arc is **thematically parallel to but distinct from
Carden's own**. Where Carden's arc moves from bureaucratic compromise to principled termination
(faith and vocation), Marelyn's moves from performative compliance to authentic self-determination
(identity and marriage). They converge at the same destination — choosing integrity at personal cost
— but arrive by different paths.

*Reassessed against the manuscript text.* The previous version stated that Marelyn's transformation
is "not contingent on Carden's" and that her Ch7 decision is "not a reaction to Carden's cathedral
experience." The manuscript supports a more interdependent reading:

- **Her decision originates independently.** "From the moment our fathers told me what happened
  yesterday, my heart already knew what we should do" (ch07:307) — that is, before Carden's
  cathedral visit and decision. Her commitment also rests on her own earlier acts: "Especially not
  after I told you to approve Annie and Roger's latest case. I promised" (ch07:325), recalling her
  Ch6 advice (ch06:116) and the Ch5 vow (ch05:272–276).
- **Its enactment is tied to Carden's choice.** "Except it wasn't ready yet for the consequences"
  (ch07:309); "When you were at Saint Alfred's, I prayed all night you'd choose to keep your job. I
  prayed you would give me an excuse not to do the right thing" (ch07:311); "Except my dear husband,
  you didn't" (ch07:313); "You made the right choice. / We made the right choice" (ch07:315–317).
- **Charles's disinheritance is conditional on her staying with Carden** — "feel free to join him in
  those circumstances" (ch07:291) — so the Winston disinheritance she files is both her father's
  punitive act and her acceptance of its price.

Read this way, Marelyn's transformation is not subordinate to Carden's — she reaches the decision
first and acts on it without him, off-page — but it is not independent of his either: Carden's
refusal to give her "an excuse" removes her last reason to hold back. The evaluation reads her Ch7
choice as the culmination of an arc that began in Ch2 ("Why can't I be me?", ch02:236), enacted in
partnership with rather than in reaction to Carden's (interpretation). The previous version's
description of her inherited identity as "the Winston name, old nobility, the purse collection as
symbol of that life" is also interpretive; the manuscript ties the Winstons to "the old nobility"
(ch04:166, ch04:190) and shows the purse collection "accumulated over their marriage" (ch07:50), but
does not itself label the purses a symbol.

**Assessment**: Meets the standard. The arc is proportionally well-distributed across the novella's
nine sections (Ch1–8 and the Epilogue) and achieves emotional resolution, satisfying the industry
standard's expectation that character-driven novellas "deliver emotional and thematic resolution"
(`industry-standards.md` §4). The supporting character's transformation reinforces the novella's
central theme (choosing conviction over comfort) without being subordinate to the protagonist's;
the interdependence identified above strengthens rather than weakens this, since it makes the
marriage itself the site where both choices are made (interpretation).

## 4. Identified Craft Risks

### 4a. Physical-Signal Saturation

The manuscript relies heavily on Marelyn's hands as the primary physical index of her emotional
state: clenching and gripping (ch04:80, ch04:98, ch04:122, ch04:264, ch07:307–309, ch08:158),
temperature changes ("terrifyingly hot," ch04:84; "cooled from burning hot to steady warmth,"
ch04:88; "ice-cold," ch04:188; "cold hands," ch07:64; "frigidly cold hands," ch07:147; "hot hands,"
ch07:331), knuckles whitening (ch04:98, ch07:309), hands clasped "as if in prayer" (ch04:274,
ch07:313), and gripping the purse strap (ch04:80, ch07:309). Marelyn's hands appear in every section
in which she is present, but the emotional-index uses cluster in Ch4 and Ch7 (counts above are from
this evaluation's search and are approximate). This is effective in isolation but risks becoming
predictable within those two chapters; the reader may begin to anticipate the hand-clenching beat
before it arrives, which dilutes its power as characterization.

A secondary saturation risk exists with Marelyn's "green eyes." The phrase occurs 14 times across
seven of the nine sections, but half of those instances (seven) fall in Ch4 alone (ch04:4, ch04:34,
ch04:56, ch04:130, ch04:144, ch04:196, ch04:214); it does not appear in Ch3 or Ch6, and appears once
or twice in each other section where it occurs. It is often paired with adjectives (inscrutable,
bright, shimmering, sparkling, glistening, half-focused) that recur with modest variation. The
previous characterization of the descriptor as appearing "in nearly every chapter" is replaced by
this count: the risk is concentrated in Ch4.

**Industry context**: `industry-standards.md` §4 identifies "overwriting" as "the most common craft
problem in fantasy novella submissions" and names "redundant description" among the faults editors
treat as disqualifying at the query stage. The novella format's compression makes repetition more
visible than it would be in a novel (interpretation). Because both signals cluster in Ch4, the risk
is chapter-level rather than manuscript-wide.

### 4b. The "Inscrutable" Descriptor (Strength with Minor Note)

Carden describes Marelyn's eyes or expression as "inscrutable" four times across the manuscript, all
concentrated in Ch2 (1x, ch02:214) and Ch4 (3x, ch04:34, ch04:94, ch04:218) — verified by search of
the manuscript files. The descriptor disappears entirely after Ch4 — it does not appear in Ch5, Ch6,
Ch7, Ch8, or the Epilogue. This distribution maps to the estrangement phase of the marriage: Carden
cannot read Marelyn when emotional distance separates them, and he stops reaching for the word once
genuine intimacy develops through Ch5–Epilogue (interpretation; Carden's misreadings themselves
continue into Ch7 — see §2b).

The natural fadeout demonstrates prose control. Rather than persisting as a placeholder for
complexity, "inscrutable" functions as a marker of Carden's limited understanding in a specific
relational phase and recedes as that limitation does. The minor note is that three of the four
instances cluster in a single chapter (Ch4), which at the chapter level may still register as
repetitive to a close reader, even though the manuscript-wide pattern is well-controlled.

**Industry context**: Agents and editors flag "inscrutable" when it appears as a recurring default
across a manuscript, signaling deferred characterization work (interpretation; this specific
practice is not stated in `industry-standards.md`, which addresses repetition under "Prose
control"). Here, the four instances are
concentrated in two chapters and the descriptor's cessation tracks the relationship's
transformation — a pattern that signals intentional craft rather than repetitive over-reliance.

### 4c. Presence in the Epilogue

Marelyn appears in all five Epilogue scenes. At the Chartwell Street construction site she pauses
"her heated argument with a municipal contractor" to watch the lattice-hoisting, answers Carden's
wink with an eye-roll and a smile (ep:8), and — as the next scene reveals — had caught the
contractor upcharging "glass sealants and weatherproof surface-resins twenty-percent above
market-value" (ep:30). In the domestic evening scene she practically inhales Carden's mutton curry
("This is sublime," ep:32), scolds him for staring at their flat's peeling wallpaper ("Stop it… You
staring at the walls won't make them better… Enjoy what you made instead," ep:34–36), goes through
closing stock-prices while Carden massages her knotted shoulders (ep:40), asks "How close are you to
self-actualizing your Calling?" (ep:42), clasps his hand and affirms "I have no doubt you'll crack
it." / "You always do." (ep:52–54), and falls asleep in his arms (ep:56). In the morning scene, the
two share a bruschetta breakfast and read the headlines together at the newsstands while she sifts
for Stock Exchange news, with an aerial fleet overhead (ep:58–76). At City Hall, she accompanies
Carden to Horace's office and elbows him afterward for indulging Horace ("Carden, how could you?
Indulging Horace's fallacies. Your own friend," ep:96), then jokes that "no woman would ever marry"
Horace (ep:100). In the cathedral, she follows Carden to the back of the congregation (ep:110),
watches him with "a sympathetic look" (ep:142), clasps his hand with "a look of worry" after his
self-actualization (ep:194), receives the deep-crimson purse he conjures from her sold collection as
his first Visionary act with glistening eyes (ep:200–204), and embraces him before the crowd reaches
him (ep:210).

This presence honors the arc the Ch8 proposal established. Marelyn is shown exercising the
professional competence (invoice scrutiny, stock-price monitoring) and domestic partnership (shared
meals, shared sleep in reduced quarters, shared morning routine) that her Ch7 transformation made
possible. The Epilogue's title ("Magic Is Self-Actualization") names a magical process — in the
manuscript, self-actualization is something an Arcanist does with a Calling (ep:42–48, ep:184–192;
glossary:73) — and Marelyn is not an Arcanist (Roger is "the only other Arcanist in their group,"
ch08:54). The title therefore applies to Marelyn only by extension: the evaluation reads her active
role at the site and her contented inhabitation of the life they chose as a non-magical counterpart
to Carden's self-actualization, which is dramatized in the cathedral (ep:156–208) (interpretation).
Her question about Carden's Calling and her stated confidence in him give her a direct dialogue role
in the Epilogue's central theme. The conjured purse as Carden's first Visionary act also provides a
symbolic coda to the purse motif, linking both characters' arcs.

**Industry context**: The industry standard expects character-driven novellas to "deliver emotional
and thematic resolution" (`industry-standards.md` §4); the evaluation reads that as including
resolution of the principal supporting arc (interpretation). Marelyn's Epilogue presence satisfies
this expectation — the co-equal partner from Ch8 is shown co-running the program (they ask Horace
"for their next assignment," ep:80), and the domestic scene gives the marriage arc a closing image
that parallels and completes the Ch4 moment when "for the first time in however many years Carden
dozed off in bed with Marelyn snoozing in his arms" (ch04:286), under changed circumstances.

### 4d. The Purse Motif: Well-Constructed with Implied Continuity

The luxury-purse collection is the manuscript's most developed character motif for Marelyn.
The purses function as:

- A marker of her old-nobility identity and its constraints (interpretation)
- A symbol of Horace's patronizing "gentleman's duty" (the indigo purse — "I just had to step in and
  fulfill a gentleman's duty on your behalf," ch02:72–76; "the blue-purse Horace bought," ch04:4)
- A target of others' judgment — Jane points at it when accusing Marelyn of courting "the
  approbation of other married men" (ch04:76–78)
- A private refuge — "her safe space's collection of luxury purses that took up an entire wall"
  (ch05:204), "accumulated over their marriage" (ch07:50)
- The sacrificial object of her Ch7 commitment (she sells her purses, ch07:277)

The motif is well-constructed through Ch7. In Ch8, Marelyn still carries the indigo purse
Horace bought her ("Horace's eyes brightened a touch at Marelyn's rich-indigo purse," ch08:114). The
Ch7 sequence shows she kept this purse separate from the sold collection: the purse wall "stood
immaculate" (ch07:225), indicating the collection was already gone, while "the exquisite indigo
purse Horace bought her last year" appears at the same dinner (ch07:265), and she clutches "her blue
purse" while explaining her choice (ch07:309).

*Corrected.* The previous version stated that the manuscript does not explicitly state her reasoning
for keeping it. In Ch8 Marelyn does give a reason, to Horace: "If we are enduring these times, we
shouldn't take the kindness of others for granted, no? Besides, you have such impeccable taste. This
purse goes so well with my wardrobe" (ch08:116). The narration frames the line as partly strategic —
she says it "in a genial tone even as she surreptitiously nudged Carden's back," and Horace's smile
"finally reached his eyes" (ch08:116–118) — so whether the stated reason is her real one is left to
inference. Her clutching it at the Ch7 dinner (ch07:309) suggests personal significance beyond
diplomacy (inference).

The Epilogue provides a symbolic closing beat: Carden's first act as a Visionary is conjuring "a
deep-crimson purse with silver buttons. One Marelyn sold last year and one of the few from her
collection he vividly remembered" (ep:202), which Marelyn receives with eyes that "glistened and
teared up" — "She blinked at Carden instead of the purse" (ep:204) — before embracing him (ep:210).
This transforms the motif from sacrifice into symbolic restoration through Carden's
self-actualization (interpretation).

**Industry context**: Motif tracking is one of the specific things developmental editors check in
upmarket fiction (interpretation; not stated in `industry-standards.md`). The purse motif's arc from
identity-marker (Ch2–5) through sacrifice (Ch7) to retention of one meaningful piece (Ch7–8) and
symbolic restoration (Epilogue) demonstrates deliberate construction. With the retained purse's
continuity shown on-page (ch07:265, ch07:309, ch08:114) and a stated, if strategic, reason given
(ch08:116), the continuity is no longer merely implied; only Marelyn's private motive remains
inferential.

### 4e. Voice Dependency

As noted in §2c, Marelyn's dialogue is often filtered through Carden's interpretive narration. In
many scenes, Carden glosses what Marelyn's words "really mean" before or after she speaks (e.g.,
ch02:218, ch04:42, ch05:250). This is structurally inherent to close-third POV, but the evaluation
judges that the manuscript leans on it more than it needs to (interpretation). Several of her
strongest lines stand unglossed — for example "Then just do the right thing" (ch06:116), "But
Carden, I was never going to divorce you" (ch07:323), and "I've walked away from all that.
Disowned, matter-of-fact" (ch08:158).

The risk: when the narration explains Marelyn's subtext, it trains the reader to rely on Carden's
interpretation rather than building their own. This makes the Ch7 dramatic-irony scene more
powerful (the reader has been conditioned to follow Carden's reads, so his misreading lands harder)
but it also means that across the prior six chapters, Marelyn's dialogue may register as Carden's
reading of Marelyn rather than as Marelyn herself.

**Industry context**: Upmarket editors look for characters who feel autonomous within the narrative
voice (interpretation; not stated in `industry-standards.md`). The test: if you extracted Marelyn's dialogue from the surrounding narration, would a reader
still recognize her as a distinct character? In this manuscript, the answer is yes — her speech
patterns are distinctive enough to survive extraction. But the narration around her dialogue
slightly undermines the reader's confidence in that distinctiveness.

### Overall

Marelyn Winston is a well-realized non-POV supporting character whose characterization meets the
craft expectations of the upmarket adult character-driven fantasy market. Her arc is, in this
evaluation's judgment, the manuscript's strongest secondary achievement — it operates as a complete
transformation narrative within the constraints of a single-POV novella, using the non-POV
limitation as a source of dramatic power rather than treating it as a handicap. The Ch7 dinner
sequence, built on accumulated dramatic irony, is the kind of emotionally intelligent scene
construction that upmarket editors and agents look for (interpretation).

Her Ch7 choice is best read as interdependent with Carden's rather than independent of it: her
"heart already knew what we should do" before his decision (ch07:307), but she "prayed you would
give me an excuse not to do the right thing" and acted once he did not (ch07:311–317). This
interdependence does not subordinate her arc to his; it locates both choices within the marriage
(interpretation).

The Epilogue honors her arc across all five of its scenes — the construction site, the domestic
evening, the morning newsstand, City Hall, and the cathedral: Marelyn exercises the professional
competence (catching a contractor's inflated invoices, monitoring stock prices) and domestic
partnership (shared meals, shared morning routine, falling asleep together in reduced quarters) that
her Ch7 transformation made possible. The Epilogue's "self-actualization" applies to her only by
extension, since she is not an Arcanist (ch08:54). Carden's conjuring of her sold purse as his first
Visionary act provides a closing symbolic beat that links both their arcs. The Epilogue shows the
co-equal partnership from Ch8 as lived daily reality.

The remaining craft notes (physical-signal repetition concentrated in Ch4 and Ch7, narrated voice
dependency, and the now-low purse-motif note) are at low to moderate risk levels. The "inscrutable"
descriptor, concentrated in Ch2 and Ch4 and ceasing entirely thereafter, demonstrates prose control
rather than over-reliance.
